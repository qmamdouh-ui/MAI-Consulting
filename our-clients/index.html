Who we talk to

Four people feel this problem. They feel it differently, and only two of them sign.

Across NGOs, foundations, UN agencies and public institutions of 20 to 500 staff. Find yourself below.

The executive director

“I need something for the board in three weeks.”

Trying to get done: Answer the board and the funders on AI with a position, not a shrug.

What hurts: A board member asked. A donor questionnaire has an AI section. You suspect staff use it and do not know how.

What counts as better: A scored baseline and a policy in force before the next board meeting. One incident fewer to explain.

How you cope today: “We'll look into it.”

The data protection officer

“I found beneficiary documents in a chatbot.”

Trying to get done: Prevent the incident, pass the audit, keep the tool list under control.

What hurts: You wrote a “don't” memo. Use went underground, and now you cannot see it.

What counts as better: A policy people follow because it permits. An approved tool list, a data classification table, an evidence file.

How you cope today: Prohibition, blocking, hope.

The HR or learning lead

“We bought a platform. Nobody finished it.”

Trying to get done: Deliver training people complete and use, and spend the training line defensibly.

What hurts: Completion in the teens. You cannot show what changed. Staff still ask what they are allowed to do.

What counts as better: Ninety minutes per department that fits a real calendar. An attendance record and a before-and-after score.

How you cope today: Renew the licence.

The head of programmes or fundraising

“Three proposals due, and everyone is using AI differently.”

Trying to get done: Get proposals and donor reports out without burning the team out.

What hurts: Uneven, private AI use. Fear that a junior sends a fabricated figure to a donor.

What counts as better: Hours back on the next three proposals — countable. One page the team actually uses. Permission to share what works.

How you cope today: Overtime and personal accounts.

Who decides, who pays, who benefits. The head of programmes feels this most and signs least. The executive director decides and usually pays; the data protection officer decides on policy and can block or sponsor; HR pays from the training line when the trigger is the training cycle. We talk first to the director or the DPO, deliver for programmes, and give HR the evidence it needs.

Training centres and academies work with us as partners, not clients. Private-sector work is taken on referral. Organisations under 20 staff are usually better served by a free course, and we will say so.

Foundations and donors are a separate conversation. If you fund organisations rather than run one, the work is a portfolio programme: one assessment across the cohort, a shared policy each grantee adapts, cohort training, and one report to you. See portfolio programmes →

How we work with you

Four steps, and you own what is left behind.
