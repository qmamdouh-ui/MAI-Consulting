The situation, with the evidence

Across the sector, AI is already in daily use for proposals, donor reports and internal documents, often through consumer tools and without agreed rules.

Sector surveys show how widespread that use is, what it costs, and how organisations currently respond. Each figure below is sourced.

Use is routine and largely undocumented

Every proposal, every donor report, every board paper. 76% of UK charities now use AI tools, up from 61% a year earlier. 92% of US nonprofits report some use, and 81% of that use is ad hoc and undocumented.

Charity Digital Skills Report 2025 (672 charities); Nonprofit AI Adoption Report 2026 (346 nonprofits).

The cost appears in three places

Only 7% of nonprofits report major impact from AI. 88% of professionals have put work information into public chatbots, a third of it customer or beneficiary data. When a funder's due-diligence pack asks about AI governance, an organisation without a policy has nothing to submit.

Shadow-AI surveys 2026; CEP AI With Purpose, 2025.

How organisations currently respond

Most tolerate it. Some purchase a catalogue licence, after which over 90% of staff still report feeling unprepared. Others circulate a prohibition memo, which moves use out of view rather than out of practice. 47% still have no AI governance policy. Among those without one, the barriers cited are training (48%) and not knowing where to start (44%).

The current response is the real alternative to this work. It has a row in the comparison table.

What the training covers

One general module for everyone, then a lab built for each department.

Both follow the four competencies of the AI Fluency framework and are taught on the organisation's own documents and approved tools. The general module sets the shared rules; each lab applies them to one department's work.

General module · all staff

The organisation-wide session

  • The rules in practice. What the organisation's policy permits, with which data, on which tools, and where to find the approved list.
  • Delegation. Which tasks to hand to AI and which to keep human; the red lines for beneficiary, donor and personal data.
  • Description. Three prompt patterns that work with the organisation's context: role, audience, constraint and format; give the source document; let the tool interview you first.
  • Discernment. The four checks before an output is used: figures, facts, language, defensibility.
  • Diligence. What to log, what to disclose to funders and beneficiaries, and who owns the result.
  • Practice. Two exercises on tasks every department shares, such as a donor update and the summary of a long document, on the approved tools.
  • Close. The shared cheatsheet, and where questions go afterwards.
Department labs · 90 minutes each

Each lab takes the general module into one department's actual tasks and ends with that department's own page. Six examples of what a lab covers:

Programmes and M&E

Donor narrative reports from field notes and the previous report; indicator wording for a logframe; synthesis of survey free-text. Red line: no beneficiary names, case details or locations below district level in any tool.

Fundraising and partnerships

First drafts against a funder's template; eligibility checklists from a call for proposals; donor research summaries. Red line: no figure accepted without recomputation against the budget file.

Finance and administration

Budget narratives from the spreadsheet; procurement comparison tables; procedure drafts. Red line: no bank, payroll or supplier identifiers in any prompt.

Human resources

Job descriptions and interview question banks; staff-facing policy explanations; onboarding materials. Red line: no candidate or employee personal data, and no screening decision delegated to a tool.

Communications and advocacy

Press releases and social copy in the house voice; translation drafts for review; briefing notes from long reports. Red line: nothing published without a named owner and a source check.

Leadership and governance

Board paper summaries; decision memos with options; scenario notes. Red line: strategic and personnel decisions are not delegated, and AI use in board papers is disclosed.

Other departments, such as legal, protection and safeguarding, IT, or procurement, are scoped the same way: a short task inventory with the team lead, then a lab on those tasks. See the page a fundraising team receives →

Inside the policy

What an AI Use Policy contains, and how a clause reads.

The policy is written as permissions, so that the work staff have to do stays possible inside it. Twelve sections, each drafted with the people it governs, approved by the board and reviewed every six months.

Structure

  1. Purpose and scope. Who and what it covers, contractors and volunteers included.
  2. Principles. The four competencies stated as duties.
  3. Permitted uses by role. A table: role, tasks permitted, tools permitted.
  4. Red lines. Inputs that never enter a tool; decisions that are never delegated.
  5. Data classification. Four classes, and which class may enter which tool.
  6. Approved tools and accounts. Organisation accounts only, required settings, review dates.
  7. Verification duties. The checks before use, and who signs off.
  8. Disclosure. To funders, to beneficiaries, in publications and in board papers.
  9. Logging and records. What is recorded, where, and for how long.
  10. Escalation and incidents. Whom to contact and what to do within 24 hours.
  11. Roles and responsibilities. Policy owner, data protection lead, department leads.
  12. Review. Every six months, alongside the re-score.
Illustrative extract · permitted use

3.4  Programme staff may use an approved tool to draft donor narrative reports from internal notes and the previous report, provided that no beneficiary name, case identifier or location below district level is entered, and that every figure is verified against the source file before the draft leaves the department.

Illustrative extract · data classification

5.3  Personal and beneficiary data is Class 4. It may not be entered into any AI tool, approved or otherwise, unless the tool is covered by a data processing agreement listed in Annex B and the use is recorded in the register kept by the data protection lead.

Illustrative extract · disclosure

8.1  Where an approved tool has contributed to a document submitted to a funder, the submission states so in the form agreed with that funder, and the responsible officer remains the author of record.

Extracts are illustrative. Your clauses are drafted from your own tool inventory, data and funder requirements. Specific annexes add rules for a department or a use where the umbrella policy is not enough.

The method

One framework underpins every service.

Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence: the AI Fluency framework published by Anthropic with Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller. The framework is public, so you can check our work against it.

Delegation

What to hand to AI, what to keep human, and who decides.

Description

How to ask, with your organisation's context already in the request.

Discernment

How to check an output before anyone relies on it.

Diligence

What to log, what to disclose, and who owns the result.

They are the four axes of your assessment score, the four sections of every team's cheatsheet, the four clause groups of your policy and the four modules of every session. The diagnosis, the training and the rules use one vocabulary, so no translation is needed between them, and a gap found in the assessment maps directly onto a policy clause and a training lab.

The obvious question

“Why engage a consultancy when free options exist?”

Use them. The two free instruments on this site are a start. Free introductory courses on this framework exist, published by the people who wrote it. Subsidised cohort programmes exist for smaller nonprofits. If one of those is the right first step for you, we will say so on the call.

Engage us for the four things a course or a cohort cannot provide: write your policy, use your team's actual tasks and documents, clear your own data rules, and produce the evidence a regulator or a donor asks for. A curriculum taught to two hundred organisations at once cannot tell your finance officer which tool is approved for which data in your organisation.

We have put every alternative side by side with its published price, including the ones that are free and the ones that would exclude you on eligibility. Compare the market →

If you operate in the EU. Since 2 February 2025, Article 4 of the EU AI Act has required organisations deploying AI to make sure that staff, and contractors acting on their behalf, have AI literacy proportionate to their role, context and risk. The obligation is outcome-based: you have to be able to demonstrate it. Department-specific training, a documented policy and an attendance record are what that demonstration looks like.
