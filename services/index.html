The situation, with the evidence

Across the sector, AI is already in daily use for proposals, donor reports and internal documents, often through consumer tools and without agreed rules.

Sector surveys show how widespread that use is, what it costs, and how organisations currently respond. Each figure below is sourced.

Use is routine and largely undocumented

Every proposal, every donor report, every board paper. 76% of UK charities now use AI tools, up from 61% a year earlier. 92% of US nonprofits report some use, and 81% of that use is ad hoc and undocumented.

Charity Digital Skills Report 2025 (672 charities); Nonprofit AI Adoption Report 2026 (346 nonprofits).

The cost appears in three places

Only 7% of nonprofits report major impact from AI. 88% of professionals have put work information into public chatbots, a third of it customer or beneficiary data. When a funder's due-diligence pack asks about AI governance, an organisation without a policy has nothing to submit.

Shadow-AI surveys 2026; CEP AI With Purpose, 2025.

How organisations currently respond

Most tolerate it. Some purchase a catalogue licence, after which over 90% of staff still report feeling unprepared. Others circulate a prohibition memo, which moves use out of view rather than out of practice. 47% still have no AI governance policy. Among those without one, the barriers cited are training (48%) and not knowing where to start (44%).

The current response is the real alternative to this work. It has a row in the comparison table.

AI Readiness Assessment

“Where does the organisation stand, and what should be addressed first?”

Starting point

A board member, a funder's questionnaire or your own staff have asked where the organisation stands on AI, and no measured answer exists. Tools are in use that no one has inventoried.

The method

One framework underpins every service.

Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence: the AI Fluency framework published by Anthropic with Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller. The framework is public, so you can check our work against it.

Delegation

What to hand to AI, what to keep human, and who decides.

Description

How to ask, with your organisation's context already in the request.

Discernment

How to check an output before anyone relies on it.

Diligence

What to log, what to disclose, and who owns the result.

They are the four axes of your assessment score, the four sections of every team's cheatsheet, the four clause groups of your policy and the four modules of every session. The diagnosis, the training and the rules use one vocabulary, so no translation is needed between them, and a gap found in the assessment maps directly onto a policy clause and a training lab.

The obvious question

“Why engage a consultancy when free options exist?”

Use them. Free introductory courses on this framework exist, published by the people who wrote it. Subsidised cohort programmes exist for smaller nonprofits. If one of those is the right first step for you, we will say so on the call.

Engage us for the four things a course or a cohort cannot provide: write your policy, use your team's actual tasks and documents, clear your own data rules, and produce the evidence a regulator or a donor asks for. A curriculum taught to two hundred organisations at once cannot tell your finance officer which tool is approved for which data in your organisation.

We have put every alternative side by side with its published price, including the ones that are free and the ones that would exclude you on eligibility. Compare the market →

If you operate in the EU. Since 2 February 2025, Article 4 of the EU AI Act has required organisations deploying AI to make sure that staff, and contractors acting on their behalf, have AI literacy proportionate to their role, context and risk. The obligation is outcome-based: you have to be able to demonstrate it. Department-specific training, a documented policy and an attendance record are what that demonstration looks like.
