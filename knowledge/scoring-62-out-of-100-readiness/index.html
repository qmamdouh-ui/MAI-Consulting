Scoring 62 out of 100: what a mid-range score means and what to do first

A mid-range readiness score of 62 can describe very different organisations, so read the ten-dimension breakdown, pick one dimension to move this quarter, and choose it using four criteria.

By M.A.I. Consulting

You have run the assessment. The number came back 62 out of 100. It is the kind of result a ten-dimension average produces often, and also the least understood, because it sits exactly where it invites two opposite, equally wrong reactions: relief that you passed, or alarm that you failed. Neither is the right response to a mid-range score, and neither tells you what to do on Monday morning.

This piece is about the actual next step, not the number itself.

What 62 does not mean

It is not a school grade, and there is no pass mark hiding inside it. We wrote earlier about what the top-line score represents: an average across ten dimensions, taken at one point in time, that compresses real variation into a single figure precisely so it fits on one page of a board report. It is not meaningfully comparable to another organisation's 62 unless both scores came from the same methodology applied with the same rigour. And it is not a prediction. A 62 today says nothing certain about where you will be in six months.

What 62 actually represents

The uncomfortable truth is that 62 could mean two very different organisations. One scores 62 because every one of the ten dimensions, named in an earlier piece, sits somewhere in the middle: nothing is broken, nothing is strong. Another scores exactly the same 62 because six dimensions are genuinely solid and four are close to zero. These are not the same organisation, they do not need the same intervention, and the average cannot tell them apart. Only the breakdown can.

The first move: read the breakdown, not the average

Before doing anything else, go and find which specific dimensions are dragging the number down. We have written about what a low score looks like on each of the ten, from fluency and delegation practice through to psychological safety and future-readiness. A 62 built on a weak data protection score is a different emergency to a 62 built on weak future-readiness, and they call for different people in the room.

The second move: pick one dimension, not three

The instinct, once the low dimensions are visible, is to fix all of them at once. Resist it. Spreading limited attention across three or four dimensions simultaneously means none of them move far enough, in the time leadership's patience actually allows, to look like progress. The usual way a 62 stays a 62 for two years running is an organisation that started five initiatives and finished none of them convincingly.

How to choose which one goes first

  • Fastest win first, if morale needs it. Fluency is usually the cheapest of the ten to move, and a visible improvement inside a month builds the credibility to ask for time on the harder dimensions later.
  • Highest risk first, if exposure is the real concern. Data protection deserves this treatment when a single uncontained mistake would cost more than years of slow, steady gains elsewhere.
  • The bottleneck first, if adoption itself has stalled. Internal politics or psychological safety are worth tackling before anything else when they are quietly blocking every other dimension from moving at all.
  • Whatever a board or funder is about to ask about. Sometimes the honestly correct priority is political timing rather than theoretical impact, and pretending otherwise wastes the goodwill a well-timed answer would have bought.

62 out of 100 is not a verdict. It is a question about where to look first.

The trade-off in picking just one

Choosing a single dimension means the other nine wait, visibly, and someone will ask why. The honest answer is that a dimension moved with real attention in one quarter is worth more than four dimensions each nudged slightly by a training session nobody follows up on. The number may not move much in the next report. The thing the number is trying to measure will have moved for real, which is the part that eventually shows up.

The practical takeaway

Before scheduling any intervention, read the ten-dimension breakdown behind your score and choose exactly one dimension to move in the next quarter; trying to move all ten at once is how a 62 stays a 62 for years.
