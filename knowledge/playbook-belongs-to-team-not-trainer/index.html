Why the playbook belongs to the team, not the trainer

A playbook written by the trainer goes stale quickly, so have the team write and own its own, updated the week an exception appears, even if the first version is rough.

By M.A.I. Consulting

We wrote before about the seven capability levels, and the highest of them, practice-shaping, was the person who writes the playbook for whoever comes after them. This piece is about who that person actually needs to be, because the instinct almost every organisation follows here is wrong in a specific, fixable way.

The instinct, once a training engagement is going well, is to ask the trainer or consultant to write it all down properly before they leave: capture their expertise in a polished reference document the team can consult afterwards. It feels efficient. It is usually how a playbook ends up dead within a month.

Why a trainer-written playbook dies quietly

A document written by the trainer reflects the trainer's model of the work, built from a few weeks of observation, not the team's actual day-to-day judgement calls. The team receives it as something handed to them rather than something they made, and that distinction matters more than it sounds like it should: nobody feels the pull to correct a handed-down document when the tool changes or an edge case appears, because it was never really theirs to correct. It quietly goes stale, then nobody trusts it, then nobody opens it, and six months later it is a file nobody remembers exists.

What makes a reference document actually get opened again

The playbooks that survive share one trait: they answer questions the team members themselves have argued about, phrased the way the team actually asks them, not the way a consultant would formalise them. They get updated by whoever just ran into the new edge case, immediately, not scheduled into a quarterly review that never quite happens. This is not a documentation problem. It is an ownership problem wearing documentation's clothes.

Four things that make a playbook survive

  • It is written, from the start, by someone who will still be here in six months. The outside consultant has already invoiced and moved to the next client; the document that outlives the engagement needs an author who outlives it too.
  • It gets updated the same week an exception is discovered, not at the next quarterly review. A playbook that only updates on a schedule is already behind the work it claims to describe.
  • It answers a question someone on the team actually asked, in the words they asked it. A document organised around the trainer's model of the domain, rather than the team's actual questions, gets searched and not found.
  • Someone specific owns it, by name, not "the team" collectively. Collective ownership of a document is, in practice, nobody's job, and it shows within a quarter.

A playbook the trainer writes belongs to the trainer's memory of the engagement. A playbook the team writes belongs to next Tuesday morning.

The trade-off in handing the pen to the team

Having the trainer write the playbook produces something more polished, faster, and ready on the day the engagement ends, and a team-authored version starts rougher, thinner, and visibly incomplete in the first few weeks. The honest answer is that the rough version is worth more within two months, because it is the one somebody keeps adding to, while the polished version is the one somebody eventually has to admit nobody has opened since the launch meeting. Polish now trades against durability later, and durability is what a playbook actually exists to provide.

The practical takeaway

Ask the trainer to facilitate the team writing its own playbook, not to write one for them; a polished document nobody owns is worth less than a rough one somebody keeps updating.
