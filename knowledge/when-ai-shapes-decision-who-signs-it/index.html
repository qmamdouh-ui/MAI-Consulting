When AI shapes the decision, who signs it? Accountability that survives an audit

When AI ranks, drafts or acts, whose signature is on it? The Influence Ladder sorts AI involvement into four rungs and three questions every signer should answer.

By M.A.I. Consulting

A programme officer asks an assistant to rank forty grant applications against the funder's criteria. She reads the top eight properly, skims the next ten, and never opens the rest. Three months later, an unsuccessful applicant asks why their proposal was not shortlisted.

Who made that decision? The officer will say she did, because she signed the shortlist. The applicant will say a machine did. An auditor will ask a third question, which is the one that matters: what, exactly, did the officer see, and what did she rely on without seeing? Accountability for an AI-shaped decision fails not at the moment of the decision but at the moment someone asks to reconstruct it.

The signature problem

Traditional accountability works because the chain is visible. A report has an author, a reviewer and an approver. AI adds a contributor that does not sign anything, cannot be questioned and may behave differently tomorrow. The person who signs is still fully accountable, but their signature now sits on top of work they only partly observed.

Two failure modes follow. In the first, the signer over-claims: "I reviewed everything", when they reviewed a fraction. In the second, the signer under-claims: "the tool suggested it", which is an abdication and will not satisfy a funder, a court or a board. Neither is dishonest by intent. Both arise because the organisation never defined what a signature on an AI-shaped decision is supposed to mean.

The Influence Ladder

We find it useful to sort AI involvement into four rungs, because the accountability each one demands is different.

  • Rung 1, Informed: AI supplied background. The human asked a question and used the answer as one input among several. Accountability is unchanged from any other research; the signer should be able to say which claims they verified.
  • Rung 2, Drafted: AI produced the first version. The human edited and approved. The signer must be able to show that they read the whole text, not the parts that looked wrong, and that they own every sentence.
  • Rung 3, Ranked: AI ordered or filtered the options. The human chose from the result. This is the dangerous rung, because the options the human never saw were eliminated by a process they cannot describe. The signer needs a record of the criteria given, the volume screened out, and a sample check of what was screened out.
  • Rung 4, Executed: AI took the action. The system sent, paid, published or refused. Accountability moves upstream to whoever authorised the automation, and the evidence needed is the boundary they set, not the individual outputs.

The rung is not a property of the tool. The same assistant can sit on Rung 1 in the morning and Rung 3 in the afternoon, depending on what the person asked it to do. That is why the rung has to be declared per decision, not per system.

Three questions that make a signature mean something

For every decision above Rung 1, an organisation can require the signer to be able to answer three questions in under two minutes.

  1. What was the question I gave the system, and what material did I give it? This preserves the framing, which is where most quality is decided.
  2. What did I check myself, and how? Not "I reviewed it", but "I opened every source cited" or "I re-derived the three numbers that drive the recommendation".
  3. What could I not see? On Rung 3 this is the excluded set. On Rung 4 it is the cases the automation was never meant to handle.

If the signer cannot answer, the signature is decorative. That is not a reason to ban the practice; it is a reason to make the answers cheap to capture at the time. A three-line note attached to the decision costs a minute today and saves a week of reconstruction later.

Nobody asks for the audit trail when the decision goes well. They ask for it when it is contested, and by then it can only be written down from memory.

Where this gets uncomfortable

The ladder exposes a tension most organisations would rather not resolve. The efficiency case for AI is largest on Rungs 3 and 4, where humans are removed from the reading. Yet the accountability burden is also highest there. A team that adopts ranking or automation because it is fast, and then keeps no record because that would slow it down, has quietly converted an operational saving into an unpriced liability.

Public and mission-driven organisations feel this more sharply. Decisions about who receives a grant, a service or a place are decisions about people, and the people affected reasonably expect a human answer to the question "why". A tool that cannot explain its filtering does not remove the duty to explain. It relocates that duty to the person whose name is on the letter.

Making it operational

You do not need new software to start. Three steps are enough for a first quarter.

  • Declare the rung. Add one line to any decision record: "AI involvement: Informed, Drafted, Ranked or Executed."
  • Set a floor for Rung 3 and 4. Require a written criteria note and a sample check of the excluded set, with the size of the sample fixed in advance.
  • Name the accountable signer for each automation. One person, by name, who can switch it off and explain its boundary.

Questions for your next leadership meeting

  • Which of our current decisions sit on Rung 3 or 4 without anyone having declared it? Start with shortlisting, triage and screening.
  • If a funder asked us tomorrow to reconstruct one AI-shaped decision, what could we produce? Try it on a real example this month.
  • Who is the named signer for each automation, and have they agreed? An unnamed owner is not an owner.

The bottom line

A signature on an AI-shaped decision only means something if the signer can say what they framed, what they checked and what they could not see. The Influence Ladder makes that requirement proportionate: light for research, firm for ranking and automation.
