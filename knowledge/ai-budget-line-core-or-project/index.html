The AI budget line: core cost or project cost?

AI spending usually lands wherever there is room. Splitting it into seats, capability and assurance gives each part an owner and a defensible home, and a plan for the year the grant ends.

By M.A.I. Consulting

When an organisation first buys AI licences, the cost usually lands in whichever budget has spare room. A programme charges the subscriptions to a project, finance absorbs a few seats into overheads, and the communications team puts its tool on a departmental card. Nobody decided that AI is a project expense or a core cost. It simply ended up somewhere.

That is manageable in the first year, when the total is small. It becomes a problem the moment the spending is worth defending, because the answer to where it belongs determines who owns it, whether it survives a funding gap, and whether anyone is responsible for what it produces.

A budget line is an ownership decision, and an AI cost without an owner is the first to be cut and the last to be understood.

Why the default answer is wrong both ways

Treating AI as a project cost feels safe. It can be charged to a grant, it ends when the grant does, and nobody has to argue for it in the core budget. The trouble is that the capability does not end when the grant does. Staff have built habits, workflows depend on the tool, and stopping it abruptly wastes the investment.

Treating AI as core overhead has the opposite flaw. It moves spending into a line that funders often scrutinise, and it invites a blanket cut in a difficult year. Worse, it hides which activities the spending supports, so the organisation cannot say what it is getting.

Neither answer is universally right. The question has to be broken down.

The Three Buckets

We find it clearer to divide AI spending into three buckets, each with its own natural home.

  • Seats. Licences and subscriptions for people who use the tools. These are recurring, predictable and shared across the organisation, and they usually belong in core costs, allocated across activities by headcount or usage.
  • Capability. Training, playbooks, champions and the time staff spend learning and adapting workflows. This is an investment in the organisation's ability to work differently. Some of it can be charged to projects that benefit directly, but the baseline belongs in core, because it outlasts any single grant.
  • Assurance. Policy, review, testing, logging and audit: the work that makes AI use defensible. It is the bucket most often forgotten, and the one that funders and auditors are most likely to ask about. It belongs in core, with a named owner.

Splitting the spend this way gives each part a defensible logic. It also stops the common failure in which capability and assurance are funded from the same short-lived project pot as the seats, and all three disappear together.

What funders will and will not accept

Funder rules vary widely, so the first step is to read your own agreements rather than assume. Some funders allow a proportionate share of shared tools as a direct project cost, provided the method of allocation is consistent and documented. Others treat them as indirect costs covered by a fixed overhead rate. Some restrict spending on training that is not specific to the funded work.

Two habits reduce friction whichever rule applies. Record how you allocate shared costs and apply the same method to every project. And describe the cost accurately: a seat that supports a project's reporting is not the same as a research tool bought for an unrelated purpose, and describing them alike invites questions.

If a funder does not allow the cost at all, that is a decision for your own core budget, not a reason to hide it elsewhere.

Show the return in the right place

An AI budget line is easier to defend when it points to what it produced. Avoid claiming savings you cannot trace. A better approach is to attach each bucket to a result the board already cares about.

Seats connect to work that was done: reports produced, translations reviewed, documents summarised. Capability connects to the proportion of staff who can perform a defined set of tasks to an agreed standard. Assurance connects to the absence of things that would otherwise appear: uncorrected errors, undocumented use, unanswered questions from a funder.

Here is an illustrative example, not a real case. A small organisation spends a modest amount a year across the three buckets. When its board asks what it gets, the answer is not "hours saved", which nobody can verify, but three plain statements: forty staff can use the approved tools, twelve have completed the role-specific training, and every use case has a named reviewer. That answer survives scrutiny in a way that an efficiency estimate does not.

If the only defence of an AI cost is a saving nobody measured, the first difficult budget meeting will end it.

Plan for the year the grant ends

Most funding is time-limited, so it helps to ask early what happens to each bucket when it stops. Seats can be reduced or moved. Capability decays slowly, but it does decay, and the loss shows up as staff drifting back to old methods. Assurance is the one that cannot be paused without creating exposure.

Build a simple continuity view: for each bucket, the annual cost, the current source of funding, the date it ends and the fallback. Review it alongside the annual budget. It rarely takes more than an hour, and it stops the situation in which the organisation discovers on the last day of a grant that a tool everyone relies on has no funding.

Questions for your next leadership meeting

  • Where does each of our AI costs currently sit, and did anyone decide that, or did it just land there?
  • Which of our three buckets, seats, capability and assurance, has no named owner and no funding beyond the current grant?
  • If a funder asked us to justify the AI line tomorrow, could we describe what it bought without relying on an estimate of hours saved?

The bottom line

Split AI spending into seats, capability and assurance, give each an owner and a home, and decide deliberately which belong in core costs. A budget line that someone chose can be defended. One that merely landed will be cut.
