The unit of AI value is a task, never a job

Why AI adoption decisions should be made task by task rather than for a whole job or department, and how a task inventory replaces the blanket yes or no.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Seven playbooks in, and every single one of them made the same implicit move: sorting a department's real work into what is worth delegating and what never should be, at the level of specific tasks, never at the level of "should this department use AI." That implicit move deserves to be stated explicitly, because most organisations get their starting question wrong before they ever reach a department playbook. They ask whether a role, or a whole job, should use AI, when the only question that produces a useful answer is which specific task inside that job is worth delegating.

This piece opens a short run on task-level redesign, the practical work of actually inventorying and sorting a role rather than issuing a verdict about it.

Why "should this role use AI" is the wrong question

A job is a bundle of dozens of distinct tasks with wildly different risk profiles. Asking whether the job should use AI produces either blanket permission, which is dangerous because it waves through the handful of tasks that genuinely need a named accountable human, or blanket refusal, which is wasteful because it blocks the majority of tasks that never needed a human judgement call in the first place. The honest answer genuinely differs task by task, and a question asked at the wrong resolution cannot produce an honest answer at any resolution.

What a task actually is, for this purpose

A task, in the sense this series has used throughout, is a bounded unit of work with a clear input, a clear output, and a specific judgement call, or the absence of one, about what happens between them. "Draft the board report" and "approve the budget" both sit inside a director's job, but they are different tasks with different shapes, and treating them as one undifferentiated lump called "the director's job" is exactly what makes blanket adoption decisions unworkable.

What changes once you redesign at task level

  • The unit of delegation becomes small enough to reason about honestly. Whether a task is safe to hand over is a far more answerable question once the task is narrow, because the risk, the judgement call, and the consequence of error are all specific rather than averaged across a whole role.
  • The same job can contain tasks that sit at opposite ends of the delegate line. Every department playbook in this series demonstrated exactly this: reconciling transactions and authorising the payment both sit inside a finance officer's job, and they belong on opposite sides of the line.
  • Redesign stops being an all-or-nothing rollout decision. A department does not need to adopt AI as a single verdict; it needs a task inventory, worked through one row at a time, which is precisely what this series' playbooks have modelled.
  • The conversation with staff changes from threat to specificity. "We are introducing AI into your role" produces fear because it is vague and total. "Here are the four tasks in your week we think are safe to speed up, and here are the three we are leaving entirely with you" produces something a person can actually evaluate and argue with.

A job is too large a unit to reason about honestly. A task is not.

The trade-off in redesigning at this resolution

Task-level redesign costs more upfront: someone has to actually inventory the tasks inside a role rather than issuing a blanket policy, and that inventory work is slower and considerably less satisfying than a single decision announced in one meeting. The honest answer is that the blanket decision is cheap precisely because it is wrong in both directions at once, over-permitting the risky tasks and under-using the safe ones, and every department playbook in this series exists because the inventory work, once actually done, is what produces guidance a department can defend.

The practical takeaway

Redesign at the level of the task, not the job: inventory what a role actually does, sort each task on its own merits, and treat "adopt AI in this department" as a category error rather than a decision.
