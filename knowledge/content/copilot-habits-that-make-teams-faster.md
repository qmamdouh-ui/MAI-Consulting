Copilot has been in the toolbar for months and most of your team have tried it once, got something generic and gone back to the old way. The licence is not the gap. The gap is a habit: people were never taught to ask for the specific thing, and nobody built the reflex to open the tool first.

This briefing draws on Mamdouh's experience with the AI Smart Work Champion programme and on several months of using Copilot day to day. It describes what to teach before any feature, where Copilot reliably goes wrong, and the responsible-use questions that should be settled early. Tool behaviour is described as observed in September 2026 and will change.

## The habit to teach first

The people who got value quickly were not more skilled. They asked for the specific thing instead of the vague thing. Three parts make up the habit:

- **Name the format before the content.** "A two-paragraph summary for someone who wasn't in the meeting" gave something usable at once. "Summarise this" gave something that needed a full rewrite.
- **Name the audience out loud.** Saying who will read it (a donor, a board member, a colleague catching up) changed the tone of the response more than any other single instruction. In the programme, naming the audience and goal first is what turned Copilot from a search bar into something closer to a colleague being briefed.
- **Treat the first response as a draft to redirect.** Follow-ups such as "make this shorter" or "that's the wrong tone for this person" are faster than starting again.

Know also what Copilot can see. It works from what is actually open and connected, not everything on a hard drive, and understanding that stopped many wasted first attempts.

Teaching method matters. Watching someone else use Copilot well did not reliably teach the habit. Training that had people type their own prompts did. Generic scenarios also never matched a real inbox or spreadsheet, and closing that gap took a few weeks of using the tool on real work.

## Why a draft beats a blank page

The productivity gap people expect is typing speed. The one that shows up week to week is the switch from generating to editing.

- Reacting to a draft is faster than producing one. Reading a rough Copilot draft and deciding what is wrong took minutes. Producing the same first version alone took longer, however clear the topic.
- A wrong draft still narrows the problem. It turns "what should this say" into "this part is right and this part isn't".
- Most people critique faster than they create, and a draft plays to that.

This extends to rewriting. People who got the most from Copilot in Word rewrote existing text more often than they generated new text. Habits that made it useful: start from something (notes, an old draft, bullet points), ask for an outline before prose, and select a paragraph to make it more concise, formal or direct.

One exception: for a genuinely new idea that does not yet exist in any form, asking for a draft too early sometimes produced a plausible answer that closed off better directions. Think first, then draft.

## Where Copilot struggles

Copilot does not usually fail by refusing. It fails by completing the task confidently in a way that looks finished and is not. Three patterns were predictable enough to plan around:

| Weak spot | What to do |
|---|---|
| Facts it was not given: a number, date or decision that lived in someone's head | Paste the source material in first, every time |
| A long document where an early detail contradicts a later one | Ask directly: does anything in here contradict anything else? |
| Tone for a specific audience; "more formal" gives generic formality | Name the actual person and what they respond well to |

In Word, long documents with strict formatting, such as a contract template or a grant report with fixed sections, still needed a human structural pass. Copilot handled sentences well and document architecture unevenly.

Because the failures sound finished, treating every first output as a draft to interrogate became the default, not the exception.

## Responsible use, settled early

Mamdouh completed a responsible AI certification and found it more practical than expected. It reframed three recurring moments:

- **Disclosure.** Be told plainly when a colleague or donor should know AI was involved, instead of treating it as a vague judgment call. That removed daily hesitation.
- **Bias.** Ask what data an output reflects rather than trusting a confident answer.
- **Sensitive data.** Have a concrete answer on what never goes into a prompt, so the boundary is usable and not merely aspirational.

The course left one question open: who is accountable when an AI-assisted decision goes wrong. The answer depends on the case, so decide it for your own organisation rather than waiting for a framework to do it. Training makes people slower to trust the tool blindly, which is most of the safety.

## What to do next

- Give every team member the same three-part ask: format, audience, goal.
- Run the training as a live session where people type their own prompts on their own work.
- Circulate a one-page list of the three weak spots and the fix for each.
- Write down your disclosure rule and your list of data that never goes into a prompt, then share it.
- Name one person who owns the open accountability question for AI-assisted decisions.
