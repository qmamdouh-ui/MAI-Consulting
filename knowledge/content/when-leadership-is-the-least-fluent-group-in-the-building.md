Every organisation we have assessed shows the same quiet asymmetry: the people deciding how AI gets adopted are often the group with the least direct experience actually using it. This is not a personal failing of any individual leader. It is a structural pattern, worth naming plainly, because it changes what training at the top actually needs to look like.

This piece is about why leadership so often ends up the least fluent group in the building, and what training upward requires that training everyone else does not.

## Why leadership often ends up least fluent

Senior staff delegate hands-on work by design, which means they see summaries and finished outputs, not the tool itself, at exactly the layer where fluency is actually built. Time pressure compounds this: learning-by-doing is the first thing to fall off a leader's calendar when the week gets busy, because nobody schedules "an hour of clumsy practice" for the person running the organisation. Status makes it worse again. Admitting you do not know how to use a tool is a small, survivable moment for a junior colleague. For the person everyone else reports to, it can feel like admitting something much larger, and so the honest question quietly goes unasked.

## Why this matters more than it looks

Leadership fluency sets the organisation's real adoption ceiling, whatever the written policy says. A leader who cannot personally judge a vendor pitch or a staff proposal on AI either rubber-stamps it or blocks it by default, because they have no basis for a genuine judgement call in between. Staff notice, and they calibrate their behaviour to what leadership visibly values and visibly understands, not to what the policy document says. A confident-looking gap at the top is not neutral. It is quietly setting the pace for everyone below it.

## Four things training upward actually requires

- **A private setting, away from the regular staff training room.** The status risk of asking a basic question drops sharply once nobody who reports to the leader is in the room to see it asked.
- **Framing as strategic judgement, not tool operation.** A leader does not need to prompt like a power user. They need enough fluency to ask a sharp question and recognise a weak pitch, which is a different, narrower skill than the one staff training usually teaches.
- **A peer or near-peer facilitator, not someone two levels below them.** Learning visibly from a junior colleague carries its own status cost. An external consultant or a peer from another organisation removes that friction entirely.
- **Explicit permission to ask the basic question out loud.** Someone has to say, plainly, that not knowing is expected in this room, or the silence simply continues by default.

> An organisation adopts AI at the speed its least fluent leader is comfortable admitting what they do not know.

## The trade-off in treating leadership as a separate programme

Training leadership separately, in private, with its own facilitator, costs more and can look like special treatment to staff who received a group session and a handout. The honest answer is that folding leadership into the general rollout usually produces something worse than special treatment: a room where the most senior people perform confidence rather than actually learn, because the setting itself makes the honest question too costly to ask. A separate programme costs money and visibility. A leadership group that never really learned costs the organisation its adoption ceiling.

## The practical takeaway

**If leadership is the least fluent group in the building, train them separately, in private, framed as strategic judgement rather than tool skills; treat it as its own programme, not an afterthought squeezed into the general rollout.**
