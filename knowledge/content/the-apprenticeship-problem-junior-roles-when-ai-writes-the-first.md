Ask a senior programme officer how they learned to write a good donor report, and the answer is rarely a course. It was years of producing weak first drafts, watching a more experienced colleague mark them up, and slowly absorbing what "good" looked like. The first draft was never valuable in itself. It was the training ground.

AI now produces a competent first draft in seconds. For organisations under pressure to do more with less, the temptation is obvious: hand the first draft to the tool, and have the junior colleague review it. The efficiency gain is real. The cost is less visible and arrives later, when the organisation looks for its next generation of senior people and finds that the route that produced them has quietly closed.

*The first draft was never the product of junior work. It was the mechanism by which juniors became seniors.*

## What the first draft was really for

Junior tasks in mission-driven organisations, such as drafting reports, summarising documents, preparing briefing notes and cleaning data, served two purposes at once. They produced work the organisation needed. And they exposed the person doing them to the reasoning behind good work: why one source outranks another, how a claim is hedged, what a funder will pick at.

AI can produce the output far more cheaply. It cannot transfer the judgement, because the judgement is built by struggling with the task, being corrected, and correcting oneself. If the junior only reviews, they practise recognising an acceptable draft without ever practising producing one. Reviewing is a real skill, but it develops slowly without the underlying craft to compare against.

We are not arguing that juniors should avoid AI. We are arguing that organisations should make deliberate choices about which tasks remain a learning route, rather than allowing efficiency pressure to make the choice by default.

## The Draft, Review, Own ladder

We suggest a simple progression that preserves learning while using the tools. Each rung is a stage in the same task, and a junior colleague moves up as their judgement is demonstrated.

- **Rung one: Draft unaided.** For a defined set of core tasks, the junior writes the first version themselves, then compares it with an AI-generated version. The comparison, discussed with a senior colleague, is the learning event. It shows what they missed and, occasionally, what the tool missed.
- **Rung two: Review with reasoning.** The junior reviews an AI-assisted draft but must record why each significant change was made. The record shows whether they can articulate the reasoning, not merely spot a problem.
- **Rung three: Direct and verify.** The junior specifies the task, uses the tool for the draft, and takes responsibility for the verification. This aligns with the delegation and discernment competencies in the 4D fluency framework.
- **Rung four: Own and coach.** The person owns the output end to end and helps others work up the ladder. Teaching is the last test of understanding.

The ladder is not a fixed timetable. It is a shared language for talking about development, so that a junior and their manager can agree where they are and what evidence would justify moving on.

## Which tasks to protect

Not every task deserves to stay on rung one. Choose a small number where the learning value is highest: those that carry professional judgement, expose the person to the organisation's standards, and have consequences if done poorly. For many organisations these include the first report a person writes for each major funder, a first analysis of programme data, and a first piece of external communication.

Routine tasks with little learning content, such as reformatting, transcription clean-up or basic summarising, can move to AI-assisted work immediately. The point is selectivity: protect the few tasks that build judgement and free time from the many that do not.

> An organisation that automates every apprentice task will discover that it has also automated its succession plan.

## What managers and leaders need to do

Three commitments follow. First, name the learning tasks explicitly, so that efficiency initiatives do not absorb them unnoticed. Second, budget review time. A senior colleague comparing a junior's draft with a tool's is doing valuable development work, and it needs to appear in the workload, not on top of it. Third, change how junior performance is assessed. If juniors are measured only on output speed, they will rationally use the tool for everything. If they are also assessed on the quality of their reasoning, the incentive shifts.

There is a reciprocal point for juniors themselves. Those who deliberately practise the unaided version of a core task, even when a tool is available, are building a durable advantage. Organisations should make that choice safe to make.

## A note on evidence

The long-term effect of AI on skill formation in early-career roles is still being studied, and we are cautious about firm claims. What is already clear from experience of any profession that automated routine work is that the loss of training tasks tends to show up years later, in the senior pipeline. That is reason enough to design deliberately now rather than to wait for the data.

## What this means for hiring

If entry-level tasks change, so should the profile sought. Candidates who can show sound judgement, clear writing and the habit of checking sources are more valuable than those whose strength is producing volume, because volume is now cheap. Interview tasks can reflect this: give a candidate an AI-generated draft with two planted weaknesses and ask them to improve it and explain why. That single exercise reveals more about discernment than a list of tools on a CV, and it tells the candidate, honestly, what the job will involve.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- Which five tasks currently build our junior staff's judgement, and are any of them being handed to AI by default?
- Where in the workload is senior time to review and coach, and is it visible?
- How do we assess juniors: on speed of output, or on the quality of their reasoning?

## The bottom line

**Protect the few junior tasks that build judgement, and use AI freely for the rest.** The Draft, Review, Own ladder lets an organisation capture the efficiency of the tools without closing the route through which its future senior people are made.

## Your next step

List the five tasks that currently build your junior staff's judgement, mark which are already being handed to AI by default, and put those back on rung one with a senior colleague's comparison time written into the workload.
