Most advice on AI adoption is written for the first year: assess, set rules, train, pilot. If the year goes well, the organisation reaches a satisfying point. The policy is signed, staff use the approved tools, and the board has seen a report. It is tempting to treat that as the finish line.

It is closer to the end of the beginning. Success brings its own set of problems, and they tend to arrive quietly in the second year, when attention has moved elsewhere. Nobody planned for them because they only exist once things are working.

*The risks of a failed adoption are obvious. The risks of a successful one are the ones organisations meet unprepared.*

## Why success is not stable

An organisation that has adopted AI well has changed several things at once. Staff have new habits, workflows depend on new tools, and expectations of speed have risen. Each of these creates a pressure that did not exist before, and none of them appears in a year-one plan because none was a problem until the plan worked.

The tools also keep moving. A workflow tested in the first year may behave differently after a model update. A policy written around one product may sit awkwardly with the next. Stability in the second year has to be maintained, not assumed.

## The Four Drifts

We describe the second-year problem as four kinds of drift, each visible if you look for it.

- **Demand drift.** Once people see what is possible, requests multiply: more use cases, more tools, more exceptions. Without a way to decide, the organisation either says yes to everything and loses control, or says no and loses goodwill.
- **Dependency drift.** Workflows quietly come to rely on a specific tool, prompt or person. When that tool changes, or the person leaves, nobody remembers how the work was done before.
- **Evidence drift.** The measurements taken in year one are not repeated. The baseline ages, the practices it described change, and the organisation can no longer say whether things are better or worse.
- **Purpose drift.** Use expands from the tasks the policy anticipated to others it did not. Nobody decided this; it happened one convenient shortcut at a time.

None of these is a failure of discipline. They are the natural behaviour of a system that has become useful.

## What to do about each

Against demand drift, create a light intake route: a short form describing the task, the data involved and the proposed reviewer, decided within a fixed time by a named person. Speed matters, because a slow route is bypassed.

Against dependency drift, keep a living register of the workflows that matter, recording for each the tool, the owner and the fallback. When a tool changes, the register tells you who to ask. When someone leaves, it tells you what they held.

Against evidence drift, repeat a small number of year-one measurements at a fixed point each year, using the same method. The point is not to prove success, but to notice change. Choose measures that describe how work is done, not how many people attended training.

Against purpose drift, review the permitted-use list at least twice a year, and add to it deliberately. Ask what people are actually doing that the list does not mention. The answer is more valuable than the policy.

## Prepare for the plateau

Most adopting organisations see a curve of early enthusiasm followed by a levelling-off. Some interpret the plateau as failure and either push harder or lose interest. It is more often a sign that the easy gains have been taken.

The second year is the point to shift attention from breadth to depth. Instead of extending the tools to more people, look at fewer tasks and do them better: improve the review step, tighten the brief, remove a handover. These are small changes, but they are where most durable value lies, and they are hard to achieve when the organisation is still chasing the next rollout.

> The first year teaches people to use AI. The second decides whether they keep using it well.

## Keep a named owner

Perhaps the most important safeguard is the simplest. Someone should be responsible for the whole picture in year two: the register, the intake route, the annual re-measurement and the permitted-use review. Without one, each of these becomes everyone's task and therefore no one's.

This does not need a new post. It can be a defined portion of an existing role, with time protected and a reporting line to someone senior. It does need a name, and it needs to be written down.

Here is an illustrative example, not a real case. A small organisation completes a successful first year and moves its adoption lead to a new project. Eighteen months later, a model update changes the way one reporting tool formats its output, and three teams discover that a template they rely on no longer works. Nobody knows who built it, or how the previous version was configured. The fix takes two days. The cost of not having a named owner was small this time; it need not always be.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- Who owns the whole picture of AI use in our organisation in year two, and is that written down?
- Which of the four drifts is most visible in our organisation today, and what evidence do we have?
- If our most-used tool changed tomorrow, which workflows would break, and who would know?

## The bottom line

**Treat the second year as a separate stage with its own owner, its own measures and its own controls for the four drifts.** Adoption is not an event that succeeds once. It is a practice that has to be kept.
