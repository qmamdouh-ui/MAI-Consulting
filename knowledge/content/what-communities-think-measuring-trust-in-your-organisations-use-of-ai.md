Most organisations that measure anything about AI measure themselves: how many staff use it, how many hours were saved, how many policies were signed. Far fewer ask the people on the receiving end. For a mission-driven organisation, those people are beneficiaries, partners, local authorities and the communities it works alongside, and their view decides whether the organisation is allowed to keep working the way it does.

Trust of this kind is not built by a policy on a website. It is built, or lost, in specific encounters: a message that reads as automated, a form that seems to ask for more than it needs, a decision nobody can explain. By the time a complaint arrives, the damage has usually been done for some time.

*If you cannot say what the people you serve think about your use of AI, you are relying on their silence, and silence is not consent.*

## Why this is different from a satisfaction survey

A standard satisfaction survey asks whether people were pleased with a service. Trust in AI use is a narrower and more sensitive question. It concerns whether people know that AI was involved, whether they feel comfortable with it, whether they feel able to say so if they are not, and whether anything happens when they do.

There are also power dynamics. A person receiving assistance is unlikely to criticise the organisation providing it, particularly if they suspect the answer could affect what they receive. A question asked carelessly will produce polite reassurance and no information.

## The Four Signals

We suggest tracking trust through four signals, each of which can be observed with modest effort.

- **Awareness.** Do people know when AI has been used in something that affects them? Ask this plainly, and expect the honest answer to be no more often than the organisation would like.
- **Comfort.** Given that they know, are they at ease with it for this purpose? Comfort often differs by task: many people accept AI help with translation and reject it for decisions about eligibility.
- **Voice.** Do people believe they could object, and do they know how? A channel that exists on paper but that nobody has used is a warning sign, not a comfort.
- **Recourse.** When someone does object, is there a person who can review the matter and change the outcome? This is the signal that turns the other three from sentiment into safeguards.

The signals work as a ladder. There is little point measuring comfort where awareness is absent, and little point in voice without recourse.

## Ways to listen that respect the relationship

The method matters as much as the questions. A few approaches suit small organisations with limited budgets.

Use short, spoken or in-person conversations in the person's own language, led by someone the community already trusts rather than by the staff member who delivers the service. Keep them separate from any decision about assistance, and say clearly that the answers will not affect it.

Involve community representatives in designing the questions. They will know which topics are sensitive, which words carry unintended meanings and which channels people actually use. In some settings, a group discussion will yield more than an individual interview; in others, the reverse.

Read what already exists. Complaints, feedback boxes, call-centre notes and messages to field staff all contain signals, and tagging them for AI-related concerns costs almost nothing.

Finally, be honest about what you cannot learn. Some people will not speak, and the ones most affected are often the least likely to. Treat a quiet result as a limit of the method, not a finding.

## What to do with the answers

Trust measurement is only worth doing if it changes something. We recommend three simple commitments before starting. Decide in advance who will read the results and who has authority to act. Commit to reporting back to participants what was heard and what will change, or what will not and why. And agree a small number of practices that the organisation will adopt or drop depending on what it finds, such as adding a plain-language notice to a form, or removing AI from a step that people find unacceptable.

Here is an illustrative example, not a real case. An organisation uses an AI tool to draft replies to beneficiary messages, with a staff member reviewing each one. Listening sessions reveal that people assumed every reply was written by a person, and that several would have preferred a slower answer from someone who knew their situation. The organisation adds a short line to each reply and offers a call-back on request. Nothing about the tool changed; what changed was the organisation's willingness to be found out.

> Trust is not lost when AI is used. It is lost when people discover, after the fact, that they were not told.

## Reporting trust upward

Funders and boards increasingly ask what an organisation is doing about responsible AI use. A trust measure gives a better answer than a policy document. It shows that the organisation has looked outward, and it produces evidence that can be reviewed. Report the four signals in a single short table, with the method used and its limits, and avoid reducing them to a single score.

Be careful with comparisons. A rise in awareness may lower comfort at first, because people notice what they had not seen before. That is progress, and worth saying so in the report.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- What do the people we serve currently know about where we use AI, and how would we find out?
- Do we have a way for someone to object to an AI-assisted decision, and has anyone ever used it?
- Who in our organisation is trusted enough by the community to lead a listening exercise, and are they on our team?

## The bottom line

**Measure trust through awareness, comfort, voice and recourse, listen in ways that respect the relationship, and act visibly on what you hear.** The communities you serve are already forming a view of your use of AI. It is better to know it.
