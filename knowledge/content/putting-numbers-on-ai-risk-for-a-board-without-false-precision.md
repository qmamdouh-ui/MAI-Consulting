Sooner or later a trustee asks the question that every risk paper is expected to answer: how likely is it, and how bad would it be? For AI, the honest answer is that nobody knows to two decimal places. The organisation has no loss history, the tools change every quarter, and the risks that matter most, such as a wrong figure reaching a funder, have never happened here before.

The tempting responses are both poor. One is to refuse to quantify, and present a list of adjectives on a traffic-light grid. The other is to invent precision: a probability of 7.3 percent and an impact of 180,000 francs, produced by a spreadsheet nobody can audit. Boards deserve something between the two.

*A number that admits its uncertainty is more useful to a board than a number that hides it, or a word that avoids one.*

## Why the usual grid disappoints

The familiar five-by-five matrix asks someone to rate likelihood and impact from one to five and multiply. It looks rigorous, but it treats a rating as if it were a measurement. Two people scoring the same risk will often differ by two points, and the multiplication turns that disagreement into a false ranking. Worse, it collapses very different risks into the same cell. A slow, frequent nuisance and a rare, severe event can both score twelve, and the board is left unable to tell which needs attention first.

None of this is a reason to abandon structure. It is a reason to choose a structure that matches how little we know.

## Bands, not decimals

We recommend a method we call Bands, Not Decimals. It has three rules.

- **Use ranges, not points.** Estimate the annual chance of an event as a band, such as "less than one in twenty", "between one in twenty and one in five", or "more than one in five". A band is honest about the state of knowledge and still supports a decision.
- **Tie impact to something real.** Define each impact band by what would happen to the organisation: a correction sent to one funder, a paused grant, a reportable data incident. Board members can then judge whether the band feels right, which they cannot do with a score of three.
- **Show the reasoning beside the number.** Record in one sentence why each band was chosen and what evidence would move it. A band without a reason is an opinion in disguise, and a reason can be challenged.

The result is a register in which every entry carries a likelihood band, an impact band, a one-line rationale and a named owner. It takes no special software and can be maintained in a shared document.

## Use scenarios for the risks that matter most

For the three or four risks a board should discuss in depth, a single band is not enough. Write each as a short scenario: what happens, how it is discovered, who is affected and what the first forty-eight hours look like. Then place the scenario in its bands.

Here is an illustrative example, not a real case. A programme team uses an AI tool to summarise field interviews, and a summary attributes a comment to the wrong district. The report goes to a funder before anyone notices. The likelihood band is moderate, because the team handles many documents. The impact band is a formal correction and a difficult conversation. The scenario also exposes something a score never would: the only check in the workflow is the author's own reading, so the control is weaker than the register assumed.

A scenario is where the board learns something. The number is only where it is filed.

## Show what would change the estimate

Every band should come with a trigger: an observable event that would move it up or down. A likelihood band might rise if a new tool is added to a team's workflow, or fall if a second reviewer is introduced. Triggers turn the register from a snapshot into something the board can watch, and they discourage the quiet re-scoring that makes a register look calmer each quarter.

> A risk register that cannot say what would change its numbers is a record of opinions, not a tool for decisions.

## What to avoid

Three practices undermine the exercise. Do not aggregate bands into a single organisational score, which brings back the false precision you removed. Do not borrow figures from other sectors or from vendor materials without saying so; a borrowed number carries someone else's assumptions. And do not present the register as complete. Say plainly which risks were considered and set aside, and which have not yet been assessed.

It also helps to keep a running note of near misses, since an organisation's own experience is the only source of local evidence. Over a year, that record will do more to sharpen the bands than any external benchmark.

## A one-page format for the board

A board paper on AI risk can fit on one page. Put the four to six most significant risks in rows. For each, show the likelihood band, the impact band, the owner, the strongest existing control, the main gap and the trigger that would change the rating. Add a short paragraph on what changed since the last meeting.

Ask the board for decisions, not approval of a document: which gap to close first, whether any risk exceeds what the organisation is willing to carry, and what evidence it wants next time. That keeps the conversation on action, which is where precision, false or otherwise, is least needed.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- Do our current risk ratings distinguish between rare, severe events and frequent, minor ones, or do they collapse both into the same score?
- For each AI risk we report, can we state in one sentence why we chose its rating and what would change it?
- Which two scenarios would we most want to rehearse with the board before they happen?

## The bottom line

**Give the board bands, scenarios and triggers instead of decimals or adjectives, and say honestly what you do not yet know.** Useful risk numbers are not the ones that look exact. They are the ones that make the next decision easier.
