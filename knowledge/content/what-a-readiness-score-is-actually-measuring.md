A board asks "what's our AI readiness score" and expects a single tidy answer, the way they would ask for a credit rating or an exam result. It is neither of those things, and treating it as though it were is where most of the confusion about these scores starts.

Before anyone puts the number in a board pack, it is worth being precise about what it actually represents, because the number survives long after the context that produced it is forgotten.

## What the number is

A readiness score is a snapshot: aggregated answers across a set of measurable dimensions of current practice, taken at one point in time, combining what staff report about their own habits with whatever artefacts can be checked against those reports. It describes where an organisation currently stands. It does not evaluate whether that is good, in any moral sense, only where the dimension sits relative to a defined scale.

## What the number is not

It is not a maturity certification with anything to display. It is not meaningfully comparable to another organisation's score unless both were assessed with the same methodology at the same rigour, which is rarer than the comparison implies. It is not a pass or fail threshold: nothing changes automatically at 50 or 70. And it is not a forecast. A high score today says nothing certain about capability in six months, because the underlying practice can move in either direction faster than most people expect.

## Why compress ten dimensions into one number at all

The honest answer is communicability, not precision. A single figure is the only version of this that fits on one page of a board report and can be tracked over time without a methodology lecture attached. The cost is that the average hides which dimension is dragging it down. An organisation scoring 60 because everything is mediocre looks identical, on the headline number, to one scoring 60 because two dimensions are strong and two are close to zero. Those are different organisations with different first moves, and the single number cannot tell them apart.

## What actually moves the number

- **Staff behaviour, not policy documents.** The assessment asks what people actually do, whether they verify outputs, whether they log decisions, not what the policy manual says should happen. A well-written policy with no adherence scores no higher than no policy at all.
- **Distribution across departments, not a headline average.** One enthusiastic pilot team and nine untouched departments can produce the same average as ten lukewarm ones. The number alone will not tell you which situation you are in.
- **A point-in-time snapshot, not a trend.** Re-score six months later and the figure can move for reasons that have nothing to do with real capability change: different respondents, a different mood in the building, one bad week for the team that happened to be surveyed.
- **Self-report plus whatever artefacts can be checked, not a verified audit.** Where a claim can be cross-checked against a log, a playbook, or an attendance record, it is. Where it cannot, the number carries that uncertainty forward silently, which is a limitation worth stating rather than hiding.

> A number out of 100 is a conversation starter, not a verdict.

## Why boards ask for it anyway

The trade-off is worth naming plainly rather than apologising for. Compression is what makes the artefact usable at board level at all: no board has the appetite for ten separate dimension scores with no summary, and no Executive Director wants to explain a methodology in the five minutes allotted to the item. The price of that usability is that the figure is less precise than it looks, and the responsible move is to say so every time it is presented, not just the first time.

## The practical takeaway

**Treat the top-line score as the invitation to read the ten-dimension breakdown behind it, never as a substitute for reading it.**
