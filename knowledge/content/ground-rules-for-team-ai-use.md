If you manage people, your job is not to use AI well yourself. It is to make sure the people who report to you do. Most of that comes down to three small pieces of groundwork: what stays out of the chat window, what people feel safe saying about their own use, and a shared set of words.

None of this needs a long policy document. It needs to be specific, said once, and easy to follow by default.

## Rule 1: Know what never goes into a chat

Most AI tools are safe for most work. The exception is not a type of task but a type of content. Once something is pasted, it has left your control, and that is the only question that matters.

**Never paste:**

- **Passwords, API keys or access tokens.** Even in a "just testing" message these can be logged, and rotating a credential is far more work than not pasting it.
- **Other people's personal information without their consent.** A colleague's medical note, a client's home address, a candidate's salary history are not yours to hand to a third-party system.
- **Anything under an active NDA or legal hold.** The confidentiality obligation does not pause because the recipient is a model rather than a person.
- **Unreleased financial or strategic details.** Draft earnings figures, an unannounced acquisition or a pricing change before launch carry real risk if they end up anywhere they should not.

**Fine to use:** public information, your own writing, hypothetical scenarios and de-identified examples. Drafting, summarising, brainstorming and editing mostly fall here and need no second thought.

> If you wouldn't paste it into a public Slack channel at your company, don't paste it into a chat window either.

The thirty-second habit: before pasting, ask who else could plausibly see this if it left the room right now. If the honest answer includes people who should not see it, redact, generalise or leave it out.

## Rule 2: Set the boundary once, specifically

- **Say what is off-limits.** "Be careful with AI" is not a policy. "Never paste client data, and here is what counts" is.
- **Name the approved tools.** People will use something if you do not say what, so choose the sanctioned option and make it the easy default.
- **Explain why.** A boundary people understand survives the cases you did not list. One they do not understand gets worked around.

A policy nobody remembers is the same as no policy.

## Rule 3: Make disclosure normal

The real risk is not people using AI too much. It is people submitting AI output unchecked because they are afraid to admit they used it. Make it ordinary to say "I used AI for the first draft", and the checking happens in the open instead of in secret.

In one-to-ones, replace "are you using AI?" with two questions: what did AI save you time on this week, and what did you have to fix afterwards? The second half tells you whether the team is using it well or just using it fast. Managing AI use well is managing trust, not managing a tool.

## Rule 4: Agree five words

Half of an AI conversation at work gets derailed by people using the same words to mean different things. You do not need to understand the technology, only to agree on a handful of terms.

| Term | Working definition |
|---|---|
| Hallucination | The model states something false with full confidence. Not a bug you can switch off but a property you check for. |
| Prompt | The instruction you give. A better prompt is more specific, not longer. |
| Context window | How much the model can hold in one conversation. When it "forgets" something earlier, it has usually fallen out of this window. |
| Fine-tuning | Training a model further on specific data. Most teams do not need it; they need a better prompt or process. |
| Model | The AI system doing the work. Different models suit different tasks, which is why "just use AI" is not a real instruction. |

Add one distinction early: AI that assists a person's judgment versus AI that replaces a decision. Most useful workplace AI is the first, and treating it as the second is where trust breaks down.

Skip the architecture, the training process and the maths. Vocabulary that does not change a decision is not worth a meeting slot.

## What to do next

- Write your own never-paste list for your team, with two or three examples from your actual work.
- Name the one approved AI tool and tell the team it is the default.
- Say aloud in your next team meeting that you used AI for a first draft, and what you changed.
- Ask each person in their next one-to-one what they had to fix after using AI.
- Print the five terms and agree them at the start of your next AI discussion.
