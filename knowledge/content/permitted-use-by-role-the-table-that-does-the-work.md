The last piece in this series argued that a permissions section has to name the task and the safeguard in the same sentence: "AI-assisted drafting of donor correspondence is permitted, provided a named staff member reviews the output before it is sent." That works cleanly for one permission. It stops working the moment an organisation has a dozen roles and several dozen permitted tasks between them, because a policy that states each one as a full sentence in prose becomes a document nobody can actually search when they need an answer on a Tuesday afternoon.

This piece is a template for the artefact that actually does the work at that scale: a single table, one row per role and task, with four columns that carry everything the earlier prose sentence carried, in a form someone can scan in seconds.

## Why a table beats prose once the list gets long

A programme officer who wants to know whether she can use AI to draft a donor update should not have to read six paragraphs of policy to find the answer. A table lets her find her role, scan down to the relevant task, and read the safeguard in one line. The same property that makes a table faster for staff to use makes it faster for a DPO to audit and faster for a new starter to learn from: everything is in one place, in the same shape, row after row.

## The four columns the table needs

Each row exists to answer one question: can this role do this task, and if so, under what condition. Four columns answer that completely; fewer than four and something load-bearing gets left implicit.

- **Role, not named individual.** A row belongs to "Fundraising Officer" or "Programme Manager," never to a person by name, so the table survives staff turnover without a rewrite.
- **Permitted task, stated as narrowly as the earlier prose sentence was.** "AI-assisted drafting of donor correspondence," not "AI use in fundraising"; a vague task column reintroduces the exact problem the permissions-first approach was meant to solve.
- **Safeguard, naming the check and who performs it.** "Reviewed and approved by a named staff member before sending" is a safeguard a reader can actually verify happened; "used responsibly" is not.
- **Data boundary, naming the classification tier the permission covers.** A permission granted for Public or Internal material, referencing the four-class scheme this series set out earlier, says nothing about Confidential or Restricted material, and the column has to say so explicitly rather than leave it assumed.

> A table only works if every row fits on one line. The moment a row needs a paragraph of caveats, it has stopped being a table and become an annex waiting to be written.

## What the table cannot do

A table forces brevity, and brevity is exactly what makes it usable, but the same discipline means a genuinely complex permission, one with multiple conditions, exceptions, or a decision that depends on judgement rather than a fixed rule, does not belong in a single row. Forcing it in produces a row that is technically present but practically useless, because nobody can act on four columns' worth of caveats crammed into a cell. The honest answer is that the table is the right tool for the common, repeatable cases, and a case that will not fit in one line is a signal to escalate it to its own annex rather than to compress it until it loses its meaning.

## The practical takeaway

**Build the permitted-use table with exactly four columns, role, task, safeguard, and data boundary, keep every row short enough to read in one glance, and move anything that will not fit into a dedicated annex instead of compressing it into an unusable cell.**
