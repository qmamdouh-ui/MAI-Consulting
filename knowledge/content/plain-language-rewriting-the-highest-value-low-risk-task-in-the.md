This series has spent a lot of pages on where AI assistance carries real risk: donor data, monitoring and evaluation, research citations that have to stand up to scrutiny. This piece is a deliberate change of register. Plain-language rewriting, turning a dense technical report or a legal clause into something a general reader can actually follow, is probably the highest-value, lowest-risk task in the whole sector, and most organisations still do it by hand, badly, or not at all.

This piece explains why the risk profile is so different here, and what separates a genuinely good plain-language rewrite from one that quietly loses something the original said.

## Why this task is unusually low risk

The source text already exists and is already authoritative. The job is not to add information, the way research is, or to interpret ambiguous data, the way monitoring and evaluation work does. It is to re-express what is already on the page in simpler words. Checking the result is a side-by-side comparison against the original, a much lighter task than tracing a research citation back to its source, which is exactly what made that earlier piece in this series a heavier lift.

## Where plain-language rewriting pays off most

Board papers written for trustees who are not specialists in the organisation's technical area. Programme reports going to a donor whose own expertise sits in finance, not in the sector's jargon. Beneficiary-facing material explaining rights, entitlements, or what a consent form actually commits someone to. Public summaries of a policy position, meant to be read once, quickly, by someone with no context. In every one of these, the reader's comprehension is the entire point of the document, and a plain-language pass is often the single cheapest thing that can be done to increase it.

## What "good" plain language actually means

- **Shorter sentences, not shorter documents.** A good rewrite says everything the original said, only more accessibly; cutting substance to make a document shorter is a different task with a different risk profile entirely.
- **Concrete nouns and named actors, not passive abstraction.** "The committee approved the budget" tells the reader who did what. "The budget was approved" quietly drops the actor, and in a governance document that omission can matter.
- **One idea per sentence.** The dense, multi-clause sentences common in policy and technical writing usually split cleanly into two or three plain ones without losing anything the original said.
- **Technical terms kept, with a plain-language gloss alongside, not silently swapped out.** Where a legal or clinical term carries precision that matters, replacing it with a vaguer paraphrase is a real loss; keeping the term and adding a short explanation preserves accuracy while still helping the reader.

> Plain language doesn't remove information. It removes the barrier between the reader and the information that was already there.

## The trade-off this task still carries

Calling this task low risk is not the same as calling it zero risk. Over-simplifying can genuinely change meaning, particularly in legal or clinical text, where the gap between "may" and "must," or a conditional clause dropped for readability, changes what the document actually commits someone to. The honest answer is that the check here is real but proportionate: a side-by-side read against the source catches this kind of drift quickly, because there is no separate fact to verify, only a comparison to make, and that comparison is far faster than the verification work heavier tasks in this series have required.

## The practical takeaway

**Treat plain-language rewriting as a default first pass for anything reaching a non-specialist reader, but always check the rewrite against the source paragraph by paragraph, because the risk here is quiet meaning-loss, not fabrication, and that risk is real even though it is cheap to catch.**
