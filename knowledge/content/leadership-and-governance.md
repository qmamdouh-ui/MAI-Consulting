Five playbooks in, and Leadership and Governance is the department every other one ultimately answers to. It is also where the delegable slice of the work is narrowest of all seven, because a named board member or executive director carries personal fiduciary and legal responsibility for the decisions this department makes, in a way none of the other departments quite replicate. A finance error can be corrected. A comms mistake is public but survivable. A governance failure can mean personal liability for a named individual under the law.

This piece sorts the department's real work the same way as the last four: what is genuinely worth delegating, and what never should be, once you take the fiduciary duty seriously rather than treating it as a formality.

## What the department actually spends its time on

Leadership and Governance lives on board reporting, strategic planning and budget approval, risk oversight, executive decision-making, stakeholder accountability, and setting the organisation's culture and values. Most of the volume is gathering, structuring and presenting information that already exists. The decisions themselves, though few in number, are where personal legal and fiduciary responsibility concentrates, and that responsibility cannot be transferred to a tool no matter how capable it becomes.

## What's worth delegating

- **Drafting first-pass board report compilations from already-approved departmental updates and metrics.** Assembling what each department has already reported into a single structured document is aggregation, not a governance judgement.
- **Structuring gathered information and known constraints into a decision brief that lays out the options.** Presenting the considered alternatives clearly is drafting work, and the board still has to choose between them.
- **Drafting routine governance documentation from approved templates.** Meeting minutes, policy formatting, and standard filings follow an established structure that a tool can populate correctly.

## What never gets delegated

- **Making the strategic decision itself, including approving a budget or a major initiative.** This is precisely the fiduciary act the board or executive director exists to perform, and the law holds a named person accountable for it, not a tool.
- **Risk and crisis oversight sign-off at board level.** The judgement about how much risk the organisation can bear, and what to do when a crisis actually hits, has to sit with someone who answers for it personally.
- **Executive hiring, firing, and succession planning decisions.** These are the highest-stakes people decisions the organisation makes, and they carry legal exposure and organisational consequence that demand a named, accountable human.
- **Modelling and enforcing the organisation's stated values and culture.** Culture is set by what leaders visibly do under pressure, not by a document a tool can draft, and staff calibrate to the former regardless of what the latter says.

> A tool can lay out the options. It cannot carry the fiduciary duty for the choice.

## The trade-off leadership carries more tightly than any department so far

Every department in this series has argued that refusing to delegate anything wastes staff time on drafting and aggregation that never needed a human judgement call, and Leadership and Governance is no exception: a board buried in compiling its own reports has less time for the judgement calls that actually require it. But the honest answer here is that the line is drawn tighter than in Finance, HR, or Comms, because those departments' worst-case failures are correctable or survivable, while a governance failure can mean personal legal liability for a named board member. That is not a reason to delegate nothing. It is a reason to be unusually strict about which narrow slice of the work is genuinely safe to hand over.

## The practical takeaway

**Delegate the report compilation, option-structuring, and documentation drafting that carries no fiduciary weight by itself; never delegate the strategic decision, risk sign-off, executive personnel decisions, or the modelling of organisational values.**
