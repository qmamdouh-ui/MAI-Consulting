The last piece in this series covered plain-language rewriting, a task with a reassuring property: a monolingual reader can still sanity-check the result, because the source and the rewrite sit in the same language and the structure is easy to compare. Translation does not have that property. A mistranslation can read as completely fluent and natural in the target language, and it is invisible to anyone who does not read both languages well enough to compare them directly, which usually means it is invisible to the organisation publishing it and to most of the people meant to read it.

This piece is about the workflow pattern that actually manages that risk: not delegating translation, but delegating the first draft and reserving translation review, sentence by sentence, for a genuinely bilingual human.

## Why translation is not like other delegated writing tasks

Most tasks in this series can be checked by someone without the exact skill that produced the output: a manager can spot a missing appendix, a colleague can catch a dropped caveat in a plain-language rewrite. Translation review needs the same specialised skill the generation did, fluency in both languages, which means the pool of people who can actually check the work is exactly as narrow as the pool who could have done it from scratch. A document wrong in translation does not fail loudly. It fails silently, in a language the people who commissioned it usually cannot read.

## What changes once you reframe the task as review, not generation

A bilingual reviewer checking an already-produced draft against the source, sentence by sentence, is doing a fundamentally faster and more reliable task than translating from a blank page, for the same reason this series found the four checks lighter than open-ended research: comparison against something that already exists catches more, in less time, than generating something new and hoping it holds up. Reframing the human's job as review rather than authorship is what makes AI-assisted translation genuinely useful instead of a fluent-sounding gamble.

## Workflow patterns for translation review

- **Assign a named bilingual reviewer before the AI draft exists, not after.** If nobody with genuine fluency in the target language is available to review it, the draft should not go out in that language at all, however confident it reads.
- **Review sentence by sentence against the source, not paragraph by paragraph.** A paragraph-level "does this sound about right" read misses the specific mistranslated clause sitting inside an otherwise fluent paragraph; sentence-level comparison catches it.
- **Flag idioms, legal terms, and culturally specific phrases explicitly.** These are exactly where a fluent-sounding translation is most likely to be subtly wrong, because a mistranslated idiom often reads as perfectly natural rather than as an obvious error.
- **Treat back-translation as a spot-check, not a substitute for review.** Translating the output back into the source language and comparing catches some errors, but it is not equivalent to a qualified reviewer reading the actual target-language text a real reader will see.

> A fluent mistranslation is invisible to everyone who cannot read both languages at once.

## The trade-off in requiring a qualified reviewer

Finding a genuinely fluent, subject-aware bilingual reviewer for every language an organisation operates in is a real staffing constraint, and it is exactly why full translation gets delegated wholesale with no review at all. The honest answer is that this staffing cost is the actual cost of operating credibly in that language; skipping it does not make the cost disappear, it just converts a visible line item into an invisible risk that surfaces later, usually when a beneficiary or a partner organisation who does read both languages notices the error first.

## The practical takeaway

**Treat translation as a review task, not a generation task, whenever the target-language reader has no way to check the source themselves: assign a named bilingual reviewer before any AI draft exists, and if none exists, do not publish the draft in that language.**
