A programme officer reads a grant report and finds a sentence that says beneficiary satisfaction rose across all six regions. The officer asks the obvious question: where does that come from? The honest answer, in a growing number of organisations, is that a colleague asked an AI tool to summarise the survey responses and the summary sounded right. Nobody lied. But nobody can say what the sentence rests on either.

This is a reporting problem before it is a technology problem. Funders have long accepted that evidence comes in grades: audited figures, sampled observations, staff judgement. AI-assisted analysis has arrived without a grading system, and the gap is being filled by silence or by vague disclaimers. We think organisations should fill it themselves, with a standard simple enough to apply in an afternoon.

*A funder does not need to know that AI was used; it needs to know how much weight each claim can bear.*

## Why disclosure alone does not solve it

Many organisations respond to this uncertainty by adding a line to the report: "AI tools were used in the preparation of this analysis." The line is honest, but it tells the reader almost nothing. It does not say which findings were touched, what was checked, or by whom. A reader cannot tell whether the tool tidied a paragraph or produced the central conclusion.

Blanket disclosure also creates an odd incentive. It is easier to attach the same sentence to every report than to work out which claims are solid. The result is a footnote that protects nobody. What the reader needs is a label on the claim, not a caveat on the document.

## The Four-Grade Evidence Standard

We propose that every substantive claim in a funder report that involved AI assistance carries one of four grades, recorded internally and stated to the funder wherever it matters. The grades describe what a person has done, not what the tool produced.

- **Grade A: verified at source.** A named person traced the figure or statement back to the original record, such as a dataset, a document or an interview transcript, and confirmed it matches. The AI may have found it, but a human confirmed it.
- **Grade B: verified by sample.** A named person checked a defined sample of the underlying items, for example twenty survey responses out of several hundred, and recorded the result. The claim is reported with its sample basis.
- **Grade C: human reasoning on AI synthesis.** The AI condensed or clustered material and a named person read the output critically and stands behind the interpretation, but did not re-trace the details. It is a judgement, and should be described as one.
- **Grade D: unverified AI output.** Nobody has checked it. It may be useful for internal exploration. It should not appear in a funder report as a finding.

The standard is deliberately blunt. Its purpose is to force a decision that is otherwise skipped: for each claim, which grade did we actually reach?

## How the grades change reporting

Two rules make the standard work in practice. First, a claim cannot be reported at a higher grade than the work behind it. If the check was a skim, the claim is Grade C, however confident the sentence sounds. Second, Grade D never travels beyond the team. If a claim is important enough to include, it is important enough to be checked to at least Grade C.

In a report, this can be light-touch. A short evidence note at the end can state that the headline figures are Grade A, the regional comparisons are Grade B on a stated sample, and the interpretive commentary is Grade C. A funder can read this in half a minute and knows exactly where to probe. It also gives the organisation something to say if a claim is later challenged: here is what we checked, and here is who did it.

## What funders can do to make this easier

The burden should not fall on grantees alone. Funders who want reliable AI-assisted reporting can help in three ways. They can say plainly which grade they expect for which kind of claim, so that grantees are not guessing. They can accept a Grade C label on interpretive material without treating it as a weakness. And they can fund the checking time, because verification is real work that rarely appears in a budget line.

Without that, the incentive drifts towards reporting the sentence that sounds best rather than the claim that is best supported.

> A claim is only as strong as the last person who checked it, and a report should say who that was.

## Where the standard bites hardest

Some kinds of claim deserve extra care. Numerical comparisons, such as changes over time or between regions, are easy for AI tools to state confidently and easy to get wrong when the underlying data are messy. Attributions of cause, such as the programme produced this result, are interpretive by nature and should rarely be reported above Grade C. Quotations from beneficiaries or partners should be Grade A, traced to the recording or transcript, because a fabricated or altered quotation is a serious integrity failure.

Summaries of literature or policy documents are another risk. A tool may describe what a document says with fluent inaccuracy. If a claim depends on what a named source states, someone should have read that source.

## A practical starting point

Choose one recurring report, perhaps the next quarterly donor update. Before drafting, agree the four grades with the team. As the draft takes shape, tag each substantive claim with its grade in a working copy. At the end, count how many claims sit at each grade. If most sit at Grade C or below, the report is telling you something about your verification capacity, and it is better to learn that before the funder does.

Keep the working copy. It is a small evidence file in its own right, and it makes the next report faster, because the checking habits are already in place.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- If a funder asked which of our headline claims were checked against source, could we answer within a day?
- Do we currently report any Grade D material as if it were a finding?
- Where in our budget does the time for verification appear, and if it does not, who is absorbing it?

## The bottom line

**Grade every claim by what a named person actually checked, and never report unverified AI output to a funder as a finding.** A funder can work with an honest Grade C. It cannot work with confidence that turns out to have nothing behind it.
