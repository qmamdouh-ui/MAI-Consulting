We have now diagnosed six of the ten dimensions, the ones that live close to a task or a document. The last four live somewhere else entirely: in how a job description quietly changes, in who has the standing to block something, in whether admitting confusion carries a cost, and in whether anyone is thinking past the tool in front of them today.

Calling these the "soft" dimensions is a category error that costs organisations real time. They are not softer to fix. They are usually the ones actually stalling adoption, while everyone's attention stays on the six that are easier to measure.

## Workforce impact: what a low score actually looks like

A low workforce impact score looks like a job description that reads the same as it did two years ago, even though half the work behind it now runs through a tool. Nobody has sat down and asked what is actually disappearing, what is intensifying, and who should be told. The change surfaces, if it ever does, only when someone asks to be regraded for doing more, or made redundant for doing less, and by then it is a personnel dispute rather than a planning exercise.

## Internal politics: what a low score actually looks like

A low internal politics score means adoption tracks who has the standing to block something, not who would benefit from it. One sceptical voice in a department, if they hold enough informal weight, can freeze a use case regardless of what the policy says is permitted. The champion trying to move things forward is tolerated rather than endorsed, which means their work carries no institutional backing and evaporates the moment they change roles.

## Psychological safety: what a low score actually looks like

A low psychological safety score is quiet by design. Staff ask a colleague privately rather than admit, in a meeting, that they do not know how to use a tool. Mistakes get corrected and buried rather than reported, which means the pattern of failure never reaches anyone with the authority to fix its cause. Often it is the fastest adopters who are most afraid of being seen to struggle, because visible enthusiasm raises the bar for what they are expected to already know.

## Future-readiness: what a low score actually looks like

A low future-readiness score looks like a training programme pinned to whichever tool was current eighteen months ago, with nobody owning the question of what happens when it changes. The organisation has optimised for today's interface rather than for the certainty that the interface will not stay the same. When the platform updates, the response is improvised, because nobody was ever assigned to expect it.

## Four quick diagnostics, one per dimension

- **For workforce impact.** Ask someone to describe their job in one paragraph, then ask what has specifically changed in the last year because of AI. If the paragraph does not change, the dimension is low, whatever the tool-usage numbers say.
- **For internal politics.** Ask who could stop a new AI use case in this department with a single sentence, and whether they already have. An immediate, specific answer is the tell.
- **For psychological safety.** Ask, in a group setting, who has made a mistake with an AI tool this month. Silence is the signal, not evidence that no mistakes occurred.
- **For future-readiness.** Ask who owns the plan for what happens when the current tool is replaced or updated. "Nobody" is the low score.

> Adoption does not stall on the dimensions anyone is measuring. It stalls on the ones nobody has named.

## The trade-off this cluster does not share with the others

The first six dimensions respond to procedure, training, and enforcement, all things a DPO or Ops lead can schedule directly. These four respond to something slower: sustained leadership behaviour, modelled consistently rather than announced once. There is no checklist that fixes internal politics or psychological safety in an afternoon, and pretending otherwise, with a single all-hands session, usually makes people more cynical, not less. The cost is real: these four take the longest to move and are the hardest to show progress on in a board pack. The pay-off is also real: organisations that move these four find the other six get noticeably easier, because staff are no longer hiding usage, blocking colleagues, or stuck defending yesterday's tool.

## The practical takeaway

**Do not schedule a training session for these four dimensions; schedule a change in how leadership behaves, because these are the ones that move only when the people at the top model something different, consistently, not once.**
