You have bought the licences and run the kickoff, and three months later most of your team works exactly as before. Take an illustrative case: adoption stalls at around a tenth of the team after licences and a single webinar. The tool has not changed. Nobody had been given a reason, a process or the time to change how they worked.

This guide is for managers and team leads responsible for the change itself. The cases in it are illustrative scenarios, not reports from client engagements. It covers why rollouts stall, how to run a first session and a first week that produce real use, how to handle a team split between enthusiasts and sceptics, how to keep the habit alive once the training ends, and how to teach checking first, train without a budget, design funded programmes for the months after, and spread a habit colleague to colleague.

## Adoption is a management job

Buying the tool is the easy step. Licensing, security review and access provisioning are concrete and schedulable, so they get treated as the whole project. Changing workflows and expectations has no deployment checklist, so it is left to sort itself out. It does not. Three things decide whether it sticks:

- **The work is redesigned, not just augmented.** A new tool dropped onto an old process mostly produces the old process a little faster.
- **Managers use it visibly.** A tool seen only in individual workflows reads as optional. One a manager refers to in a team meeting reads as expected.
- **There is a specific first use case.** "Use AI more" gives nobody anything to act on. "Draft your first pass of the weekly report with it" gives them something to try by Friday.

A tool nobody was shown how to use gets used by whoever was already inclined to explore. Everyone else waits for a permission that never quite arrives.

## Why passive training fails

In another illustrative case, ninety people sit through ninety minutes of AI training, rated it highly in a satisfaction survey, and changed nothing about how they worked. The session was not badly built. It asked people to watch and remember, and watching has never changed a habit. A session people enjoyed and a session that changed behaviour are graded on different scales.

Behaviour changes when:

- **People practise on their own real work**, not a demo. A sample task shows what is possible. Your own report shows that it is usable.
- **A task is due before the memory fades.** A skill untouched for two weeks might as well not have been taught.
- **Someone checks what people did.** No follow-up signals, correctly, that using the tool is optional.

Passive sessions keep getting scheduled because they are easy to calendar, easy to mark complete and easy to report as "training delivered."

## The thirty-minute first session

A team lead expecting to run a two-hour slide workshop instead gave everyone one real task from that week, thirty minutes, and one rule: use the tool, then write down one thing it got right and one thing it got wrong. By the end, five people had five concrete lessons instead of one generic memory.

It works because it uses real work, ends with something written down, and is short enough to finish. A two-hour session mostly covers features nobody will use in the first month on tasks nobody has. Thirty minutes forces the one thing that matters at the start: doing a real task and noticing what happened.

## The one-week exercise

A manager whose team kept avoiding the tool, not from dislike but because nobody had shown how it applied to their work, skipped another training session. She gave the team one real task due Friday and asked them to report what worked and what did not. That week closed more of the gap than the rollout had.

- **Use a real deliverable with a deadline that already exists.** Made-up exercises are easy to deprioritise.
- **Compare outputs.** Have people show what they produced with the tool beside what they would have produced without it, so "it helped" becomes a visible before and after.
- **Hold a debrief.** A twenty-minute share-out spreads five people's learning to everyone.

A day is too short for the tool to succeed or fail visibly. A quarter loses urgency. A week is long enough to reach a real deliverable and short enough to stay in mind.

## When the team is split

Another manager found half her team experimenting on their own time and half who had never opened the tool. A single session aimed at the middle satisfied nobody. Treat it as two audiences:

- **Enthusiasts need a harder problem.** A basic walkthrough bores them. A real task where the tool's limits show keeps them engaged.
- **The resistant half is usually unconvinced or under-supported**, not anti-technology. Common causes are a bad first experience, no visible slack for learning, or real doubt the tool applies to their work. Someone else's enthusiasm answers none of these.
- **Peer proof beats management proof.** A sceptical colleague is more persuaded by a result from the next desk than by the same case made from the front of the room.

Pair an enthusiast with a resistant colleague on one shared, real task. The enthusiast gets a harder test of the tool. The sceptic gets proof, on work they recognise, from someone with nothing to sell.

## Keep the habit after the training

In a further example, a well-reviewed hands-on session was followed three months later by an audit of actual use showing most people had drifted back to their old ways. The training worked as training. It did not survive an ordinary Tuesday, for three reasons: the training task was not the person's real task, old habits are automatic and win under deadline pressure, and nobody checked back.

The teams that kept using a tool built a small repeated moment into an existing workflow: a recurring five-minute check on one recurring task. It makes the behaviour happen regardless of motivation that week. Measure what people do three months on, not attendance and satisfaction scores.

## Teach the checking habit before the buttons

One team spent a full training day on which buttons to click in a new tool and no time on checking its output. Two weeks later a report went out with a fabricated statistic nobody had caught. The interface was easy to teach. The habit that would have stopped the error was skipped.

- **The tool will change; the habit will not.** Checking output against something the person actually knows transfers to every version and every future tool.
- **Interface confidence hides the risk.** Someone who learns the buttons quickly looks trained, which makes it easy to skip the slower work of teaching them to question what comes back.
- **A skipped check surfaces late and expensively,** in a report, a client email or a grant application, not in the training room.

The habit itself is small: before anything a tool produces goes out, someone who knows the subject reads it and asks whether it matches what they know to be true. Teaching it takes about ten minutes. Do it before anyone is shown where the buttons are.

## No IT team and no budget: borrow time you already have

A regional development nonprofit wanted every programme officer using AI tools well within a quarter, with no IT department, no training budget and nobody whose job was to teach software. The programme director ran fifteen-minute sessions inside existing team meetings, and within the quarter most of the team had a working habit.

- **One task per session, tied to real work.** "How to draft this week's donor email with AI" trains the room; "how AI works" trains nobody.
- **Borrow existing meeting time.** Fifteen minutes carved out of a weekly meeting is an easier sell than a new recurring slot, and it is harder to let lapse than a standalone programme that looks like a special initiative.
- **Let the fastest learner run the next demo.** Ownership spreads beyond the one person who started it, which matters once that person moves on or gets busy.

## Funded programmes: design the ninety days after the workshop

A donor funded a two-day AI workshop for twelve partner organisations, with slides, a certificate and a follow-up survey. Attendance and scores were good. Six months later almost none of the twelve had a working AI habit. The workshop taught AI; nothing was built to keep people using it once the trainer left.

- **Optimise for the ninety days after, not the training day.** A happy room is easy to report and predicts little about a normal week back at the office.
- **Teach one recurring task, not the tool in general.** A broad tour produces enthusiasm and no next action.
- **End with a check-in, not only a certificate.** A short follow-up a month later, asking what people used and what got in the way, catches the drop-off while there is time to act. A scheduled call, a shared channel or a peer pairing is enough, but it has to be designed in from the start.

The same logic applies to inclusion programmes. A vocational training centre gave every graduate free access to a leading AI tool, and six months later the graduates using it well were the ones who already had strong digital literacy. Access had given an existing advantage a new outlet. The next intake got the same tool plus explicit instruction in how to ask it useful questions, and placement outcomes changed. People with less confidence or less prior exposure ask vaguer questions, use the tool hesitantly, and stick to the one use they can already imagine. Treat directing the tool as the skill being taught: structured practice on personally relevant tasks, feedback on why one request worked better than another, and enough repetition that a good question becomes habit.

## Teach one colleague on a real task

The largest jump in a team's output rarely comes from a better prompt. It comes from one person spending twenty minutes showing a colleague how they draft, check and send AI-assisted work. A personal habit has a ceiling of one person's week; a taught habit also removes the bottleneck where every request routes through the one person who knows how. Explaining it aloud exposes which steps were real judgement calls. No slides were needed: they sat together, opened a real task and narrated each decision, what to delegate, how to phrase the request, what to check before it went out.

## What to do next

- Name one specific first use case per person, tied to a deliverable already on their calendar.
- Replace any long kickoff with a thirty-minute session on real work that ends with one thing right and one thing wrong, written down.
- Set a one-week exercise with a Friday deadline and book the twenty-minute debrief now.
- Split your team into two conversations, and pair one enthusiast with one sceptic on a shared task.
- Add a recurring five-minute review of one recurring task, and check actual use at three months.
- Teach the ten-minute checking habit before any interface training.
- If you have no training budget, take fifteen minutes from an existing weekly meeting and teach one real task.
- If you fund or run a programme, schedule the one-month check-in before the workshop happens.
- Ask your most fluent person to spend twenty minutes narrating a real task to one colleague this week.
