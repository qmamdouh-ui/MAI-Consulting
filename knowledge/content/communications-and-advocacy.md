Three playbooks in, and Communications and Advocacy raises a question the previous two departments did not have to answer quite so sharply: what happens once the mistake is published. Finance can catch an error before the payment clears. HR can correct a bad process before the next hire. Comms publishes into public view, and the moment something goes out under the organisation's name, taking it back is rarely as simple as deleting the post.

This piece sorts the department's real work the same way as the last two: what is genuinely worth delegating, and what never should be, once you take seriously that a comms mistake is a public one.

## What the department actually spends its time on

Communications and Advocacy lives on drafting social media posts and newsletters, monitoring media coverage and social sentiment, translating technical or policy content for public audiences, coordinating with journalists and partner organisations, and taking positions on the issues the organisation campaigns on. Most of it is drafting and aggregation against messaging that has already been agreed. A narrow but consequential slice of it is the moment the organisation's name is attached to a public statement, or a relationship with an external party is handled with judgement a tool cannot supply.

## What's worth delegating

- **Drafting first-pass social media posts and newsletter copy from approved talking points.** Turning agreed messaging into platform-ready copy is drafting against known inputs, and a human still reviews it before anything goes out.
- **Summarising media coverage and social listening data into a themed weekly digest.** Pulling patterns out of what has already been said about the organisation is aggregation, not a judgement about how to respond to any of it.
- **Producing first-draft translations or format adaptations of already-approved content.** Once a position is approved in one language or format, adapting it into another is drafting work, not a new position.
- **Scheduling and formatting routine content across channels.** Getting approved content into the right place at the right time is logistics with no communications judgement attached to it.

## What never gets delegated

- **Responding to a reputational crisis or a fast-moving public controversy.** The judgement about what to say, when, and to whom carries organisational risk that has to be weighed by someone who can be held accountable for the call, not generated by a tool working from a prompt.
- **Taking a public position on a sensitive political or policy matter.** Advocacy positioning commits the organisation's name and its relationships to a stance, and that commitment needs a named, accountable person standing behind it.
- **Any direct, sensitive exchange with a journalist, government official, or partner organisation.** These exchanges rely on trust, discretion, and a relationship history a tool has no access to, and a misjudged reply here cannot be quietly withdrawn once it has been read.

> A tool can draft the message. It cannot decide what the organisation stands for.

## The trade-off comms carries differently from the other two

Finance and HR both carry real consequences for getting delegation wrong, but an internal error usually stays inside the organisation long enough to be corrected before anyone outside notices. Comms does not get that grace period: the moment a post, statement or reply is public, the organisation is answering for it in real time, in front of the audience it was trying to reach. Refusing to delegate anything, out of fear of exactly this, leaves comms staff drowning in drafting and scheduling that never needed a human judgement call. The honest answer is that the line here is drawn around publication and public commitment, not around effort: drafting and aggregating carries no exposure by itself, and the exposure only starts at the point something is said in the organisation's name to an audience it cannot un-hear it from.

## The practical takeaway

**Delegate the drafting, aggregation, and scheduling that produces no public commitment by itself; never delegate a crisis response, a policy position, or a sensitive direct exchange with an external stakeholder.**
