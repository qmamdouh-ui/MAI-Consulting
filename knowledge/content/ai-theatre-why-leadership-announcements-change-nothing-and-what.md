There is a familiar sequence. A leader announces that the organisation is "embracing AI". A tool is licensed. A launch event is held, a channel is created, and a champion posts a few enthusiastic examples. Six weeks later, the channel is quiet and the work is done exactly as before, except that a few people are using the tool privately and a few are avoiding it deliberately.

We call this AI theatre: visible activity that produces the appearance of adoption without changing how anyone works. It is rarely cynical. Most leaders who stage it sincerely believe the announcement is the first step. *The mistake is treating the announcement as the intervention, when it is only the trailer.*

## Why announcements fail

People do not change how they work because they were told to. They change when three things are true at once: they know what is now allowed, they have time and a reason to try, and they see that people above them do it too. An announcement supplies the first at best, in vague terms. It supplies none of the second and third.

There is also a subtler cost. When a public commitment is not followed by visible change, staff learn that leadership statements on AI are decorative. That teaches them to discount the next one, which makes real change harder than if nothing had been said. The 2026 survey of 346 nonprofits on our website found 81% of AI use was individual, with no shared workflows. That is what a well-announced, poorly embedded programme looks like: enthusiasm at the top, private improvisation below.

## Three layers: Announce, Enable, Embed

Our diagnostic sorts adoption effort into three layers. Theatre is what happens when an organisation invests only in the first.

- **Announce: the message.** Vision statements, launch events, town halls. Necessary, cheap, and by itself close to worthless. Its job is to give permission and set direction, not to change behaviour.
- **Enable: the conditions.** Approved tools, a clear permitted-use rule by role, protected time to practise, and someone to ask. This is where most organisations stop, and where most training budgets end up.
- **Embed: the practice.** Changed routines in real work, visible to the people around it: a team meeting that starts from an AI-assisted draft, a review step that includes checking the machine's output, a metric that someone looks at monthly.

A useful test is to ask of any initiative which layer it serves. A launch event serves Announce. A policy serves Enable. Only a changed routine serves Embed, and Embed is the only layer that leaves evidence in the work itself.

## What leaders can do that a message cannot

The behaviours that move an organisation are small and slightly uncomfortable, which is why they are rare.

- **Use it on your own work, in view.** A director who says "I drafted this with an assistant and rewrote two sections because they were wrong" gives more permission than any policy. It shows the practice, the checking and the ownership in one sentence.
- **Show the correction, not only the success.** Leaders who share only wins signal that mistakes are unwelcome, which drives use underground. Sharing an error, and how it was caught, makes the checking habit normal.
- **Protect the time.** If practice happens only after hours, the message is that AI is a hobby. Removing one recurring meeting to fund practice time is a stronger signal than any speech.
- **Publish your own red lines.** Say which of your own tasks you will not delegate, and why. Staff calibrate against what leaders will not do.
- **Ask a different question in reviews.** Replace "are you using AI?" with "what did you delegate this week, and what did you keep?" The first invites performance. The second invites description.

> If the people who announced the programme cannot describe how they used it last week, the programme is a broadcast.

## Signals that separate theatre from change

Boards and executives can look for evidence that does not depend on anyone's enthusiasm.

1. **Shared artefacts exist.** Prompts, checklists or workflows that more than one person uses and improves. Individual cleverness does not count.
2. **Verification appears in the process.** A review step that mentions checking AI output, with a named person. If reviews do not mention it, either it is not being used or it is not being checked.
3. **The questions have changed.** Staff ask "which of these tasks should we delegate?" rather than "am I allowed?" The first shows practice, the second shows uncertainty.
4. **Someone has stopped something.** A report no longer produced, a step removed. Adoption that changes nothing being done has not changed anything.

We would add a warning about metrics. Counting logins or prompts rewards activity, not change, and teams will optimise for whatever is counted. A better indicator is one measured before-and-after on a real piece of work: how long it took, how much rework it needed, and who checked it.

## A note on fairness

It is easy to read this as a criticism of leaders. It should be read as a criticism of the default sequence, in which the cheapest layer goes first and the hardest is left to the enthusiasts. Leaders are usually busy, are given little guidance on what visible practice looks like, and are often more cautious than their staff because they see the consequences of errors. The remedy is not exhortation. It is a short, specific list of behaviours, agreed and repeated.

## Questions for your next leadership meeting

- **Which layer did our last AI initiative serve: Announce, Enable or Embed?** If the answer is Announce, name the next Embed step.
- **What did each of us delegate to AI last week, and what did we deliberately keep?** Try answering aloud, in the meeting.
- **What have we stopped doing because of AI?** If nothing, we may be adding rather than changing.

## The bottom line

**Announcements grant permission, but only visible practice changes behaviour, so leaders should spend less on the launch and more on using AI on their own work, in view, with the checking shown.** The three layers, Announce, Enable and Embed, give a fast way to see which part of the effort is missing.
