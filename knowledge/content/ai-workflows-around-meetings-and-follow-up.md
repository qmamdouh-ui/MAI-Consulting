A call lands with ten minutes' notice, a workshop ends with forty sticky notes and no plan, a promising conversation needs a follow-up that never quite gets written. The work around a meeting is where momentum is lost, because it is rarely scheduled and always rushed.

Four illustrative scenarios show how a few well-chosen requests close those gaps. Each ends with something only you can supply, and that is worth planning for.

## Before: prepare in the time you have

### The ten-minute meeting prep

A client call arrives with ten minutes' notice and no time to reread the account history. Twenty minutes of scrolling can shrink to the time it takes to walk to the room. You paste in the last few email threads and ask for three things:

- A one-paragraph summary of where the relationship stands.
- The three topics most likely to come up, based on recent history.
- One specific, well-informed question to lead with, which shows you know the history.

The point is not to know everything. It is not to be caught out by the one thing you should have remembered. Treat this as a floor. If the meeting is about a proposal, you still need to read the proposal.

### A training session in under an hour

A new hire needed a walkthrough that usually got a half-day workshop, with an hour to prepare and no materials to reuse. The structured hour came from three requests:

- Start from the outcome: what should this person be able to do by the end of the hour?
- Build in a real check partway through, a scenario or question the learner must respond to.
- List what the session deliberately will not cover, so it is honest about its limits.

The scenario was rewritten by hand because it needed a detail from the learner's actual role. A check-in question cannot be fully generic. The best hour-long session is built for the hour it has, not cut down from the full one.

## After: close the loop while it is fresh

### The two-minute follow-up

A good call ended with three action items and a vague promise to follow up soon, and soon usually meant when there was time to write something professional. The two-minute version, which makes same-day sending far more likely:

- Start from three rough bullets from the call, not a blank subject line.
- Ask for the email to open with the next step, not a recap, so it works for someone skimming on a phone.
- Ask for the tone to match the conversation, as warm or as brisk as the call itself.

You then add by hand the small detail that shows you listened, such as an offhand comment about a shared connection. The email does not need to be memorable. It needs to exist while the conversation still does.

### From workshop notes to action plan

A two-day strategy workshop ended with whiteboard photos, a disorganised shared document and about forty sticky notes of ideas everyone liked and nobody had turned into next steps. Closing that gap used to take a week. The afternoon version ran like this:

1. Transcribe everything into one raw document, unsorted, before trying to make it look like a plan.
2. Ask what patterns appear across the ideas, rather than assuming the themes. This surfaced groupings the room had not noticed while generating them.
3. Ask for one owner and one concrete next step per theme, which is what makes the plan usable on Monday.

The facilitator still had to judge. Two themes that looked separate were one underlying issue in different language, and only someone who had been in the room reading the group's energy would have merged them.

## What the four have in common

| Moment | Ask for | Add yourself |
|---|---|---|
| Prep | Status, likely topics, one good question | The proposal, if it matters |
| Training | Outcome, a check, what is out of scope | A scenario from the learner's role |
| Follow-up | Next step first, matching tone | A personal detail |
| Workshop | Themes, one owner and one action each | Merging themes that are really one |

In every case the request is short and specific, and the output is something you can act on within minutes.

## What to do next

- Before your next call, paste in the last three threads and ask for the status paragraph, three likely topics and one question.
- Draft your next follow-up from three bullets within an hour of the call ending.
- When you have to teach something quickly, write the end-of-hour outcome first and ask for a mid-session check.
- After your next workshop, put every note into one document and ask for themes, owners and next steps before you tidy anything.
