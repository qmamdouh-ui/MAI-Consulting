Training breadth vs behavioural depth: the trade-off nobody prices

Spreading training thinly across everyone gives a good completion rate but rarely moves anyone past guided use, while depth costs visibility; here is how to decide where the hours go.

By M.A.I. Consulting

We wrote last time about the seven capability levels and why Level 3, independent competent use, is the floor below which nothing durable has happened yet. The obvious next question, in any budget conversation, is where the limited training hours actually go: spread thin across everyone, or concentrated on fewer people until they cross that floor. Most training plans quietly avoid answering this question and default to breadth, because breadth is the answer nobody has to defend in a room.

This piece is about naming that default for what it is, and about the cost that comes with each side of the choice, because both sides have one.

Why breadth is the default nobody has to defend

Training everyone a little produces a completion percentage that looks good in a board pack, treats every department as equally deserving, and avoids the genuinely uncomfortable conversation about which team gets the deeper, more expensive investment first. None of this is dishonest. It is simply easier to announce "eighty per cent of staff completed AI awareness training" than to explain why finance got three days of hands-on coaching and the rest of the organisation got a ninety-minute session.

What breadth actually buys you

A wide, shallow rollout genuinely does something: it lowers fear across the organisation, builds a shared vocabulary so people can at least discuss the tools without embarrassment, and reduces the resistance the next, deeper phase of training will meet. What it does not do is move anyone past guided use. Breadth training, honestly assessed, produces Level 2 at best, and Level 2 is the level that evaporates the moment the trainer leaves the room.

What depth actually buys you

Concentrating the same hours on a smaller group, until they reach Level 3 and beyond, produces something breadth cannot: people who can do the actual work correctly, alone, and who can then unblock a colleague without escalating to the champion. A department with three genuinely competent people spreads capability sideways in a way that thirty people at guided use never do, because the three can teach, and the thirty at guided use have nothing durable to pass on.

Four questions that decide where the budget actually goes

  • How many people in this department touch the relevant task daily, not occasionally? High daily volume justifies the cost of depth; low volume rarely does.
  • Is there already someone at Level 3 or above who could carry the department once trained further? An existing near-competent person is the cheapest path to a genuine multiplier.
  • Can this quarter's board report survive a smaller, less impressive completion number in exchange for a real capability? If not, be honest that the choice is political, not developmental.
  • Can next quarter's board report survive a rollout that reached everyone and changed nothing? This is the question breadth-only plans rarely get asked, and the one they should fear most.

Breadth trains everyone to nod along. Depth trains someone who can actually do the work when the trainer has left the room.

The trade-off itself, named plainly

Depth training excludes people, visibly, in a way that invites a fair question from everyone not chosen: why not me. Breadth training excludes no one and answers that question before it is asked, at the cost of producing nothing that survives past the training session for anyone. The honest answer is that both costs are real, and an organisation that pretends otherwise, by calling breadth training "capability building" in a board report, is spending real money on a number that will not hold up when someone tests it in six months.

The practical takeaway

Spend most of the training budget on depth for the department that will use the capability daily, and be explicit that broader awareness training across the rest of the organisation is building vocabulary and reducing fear, not building capability; do not let a board report conflate the two.
