The attendance record: trivial to keep, impossible to reconstruct

A training attendance record takes thirty seconds to keep and cannot be rebuilt later, so capture who attended, when, what was covered and at what level, including contractors.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Every training session produces one piece of evidence nobody thinks about until they need it: a record of who was actually in the room, when, and what was covered. Keeping it at the time takes a spreadsheet row and thirty seconds. Reconstructing it eighteen months later, when someone finally asks for proof, is often simply not possible.

This is not a hypothetical concern. The EU AI Act's Article 4 became applicable on 2 February 2025, and it asks organisations to support the AI literacy of staff and of others acting on their behalf, a category that, as we wrote before, includes contractors. An attendance record is the cheapest, plainest piece of evidence that this obligation is actually being met, and it is exactly the kind of unglamorous record most training budgets never think to protect.

Why the record is trivial to keep in the moment

At the point of delivery, the information already exists: the trainer knows who is in the session, the calendar invite shows the date, the agenda shows what was covered. Capturing it costs almost nothing, a single spreadsheet row per attendee, because every input is already sitting in front of whoever is running the session.

Why it is nearly impossible to reconstruct later

Eighteen months on, the trainer may have moved on, the calendar invite may have been deleted, and the agenda may exist only as a memory of what was probably covered. Nobody can honestly reconstruct who attended a specific session from memory, and a reconstructed record built after the fact is not evidence, it is a guess dressed up as a record. Once the moment passes, the option to capture it cheaply is gone permanently, and no amount of effort later buys it back.

What a bare-minimum attendance record actually needs

  • Who was there, by name, including contractors and other people acting on the organisation's behalf. Article 4 covers this wider group explicitly, and a record that only lists employees misses exactly the people most often overlooked.
  • When the session happened, to the day. A vague "sometime last spring" is not verifiable and will not hold up if anyone ever asks for the actual date.
  • What was covered, in enough detail that a stranger could tell whether it was substantive or a five-minute mention in a longer meeting. An agenda line is enough; a full transcript is not required.
  • What level the session was pitched at, so it is later possible to tell a genuine capability-building session from an awareness-only briefing that never claimed to do more.

The record costs a spreadsheet row today. It costs nothing tomorrow because tomorrow it no longer exists to capture.

The trade-off in bothering with this at all

Keeping the record properly costs a small, real amount of administrative discipline every single session, applied by someone who has to remember to do it when there is no immediate reward for doing so. The honest answer is that this discipline is cheap precisely because it is spread thinly over many small moments, while the alternative, needing the record and not having it, concentrates the entire cost into one moment when it is needed most and cannot be paid for at any price. A discipline that costs little every time is nearly always worth more than a shortcut that costs nothing until the one day it costs everything.

The practical takeaway

Capture who attended, when, what was covered, and at what level, in a single spreadsheet row, at the time of every training session, including for contractors; the thirty seconds it costs now is the only version of this evidence that will ever exist.
