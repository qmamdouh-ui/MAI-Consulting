AI playbook for HR: the department where most red lines live

Which HR tasks can be delegated to AI, such as scheduling, job posting drafts and aggregated survey themes, and why hiring, discipline, grievances and individual personal data stay with named humans.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Two playbooks in, a pattern is already visible: every department has real work worth delegating and real work that should never be handed to a tool. HR is different in degree, not in kind. It has fewer tasks worth delegating than Finance and Administration, and more that sit firmly in the never column, because almost everything HR touches is tied to a specific person's livelihood, and several jurisdictions attach real legal liability to how that person was judged.

This piece sorts HR's real work the same way as the last two: what is genuinely worth delegating, and what never should be, whatever the time pressure looks like.

Why HR carries more red lines than most departments

HR lives on recruitment, onboarding, performance reviews, disciplinary processes, grievance handling, payroll administration shared with Finance, and exit interviews. A meaningful share of that work involves ranking or judging a named individual against other named individuals, often for an outcome, a job, a promotion, a warning, that has real consequences for that person's income and reputation. Discrimination law in most jurisdictions holds the organisation accountable for how that judgement was made, and a tool cannot be named as the accountable party.

What's worth delegating

  • Drafting job postings and role descriptions from an approved template and the hiring manager's brief. Turning a brief into a formatted posting is drafting against known inputs, and a human still approves the final wording before it goes live.
  • Scheduling and coordinating interview logistics across candidates and panellists. Calendar coordination carries no judgement about any individual and is exactly the kind of task that should never eat a recruiter's afternoon.
  • Summarising anonymised, aggregated themes from exit interviews or engagement surveys across many respondents. Once responses are aggregated and stripped of anything that identifies an individual, pattern-spotting across a large dataset is a mechanical task, not a judgement about one person.

What never gets delegated

  • Deciding who gets shortlisted, interviewed, or offered a job. Screening tools have a well-documented history of encoding bias from historical hiring data, and the accountable decision about a specific candidate has to sit with a named human who can answer for it.
  • Any disciplinary action or termination decision. These carry direct legal and financial consequences for the employee and the organisation, and that accountability cannot transfer to a tool that will never sit in front of an employment tribunal.
  • Investigating or adjudicating a harassment, discrimination, or grievance complaint. Reading a complaint correctly requires context a tool does not have access to, and misreading one risks missing a genuine safeguarding issue or wrongly escalating an ordinary disagreement.
  • Handling an individual employee's protected personal data tied to their specific circumstances, such as health information, disability accommodations, immigration status, or an individual pay equity case. We have written before about the four-class data table: this is exactly the class of data that never touches a general-purpose tool.

A tool can draft the job posting. It cannot decide who gets the job.

The trade-off HR cannot fully escape

Refusing to delegate anything in HR, out of an abundance of caution, means recruiters and HR administrators spend real hours on scheduling and drafting that never needed a human judgement call in the first place, and that cost compounds across every open role and every review cycle. The honest answer is that HR's red lines are drawn where they are because discrimination and employment law hold a human accountable for decisions about named individuals, not because today's tools are incapable of producing a plausible-looking ranking or recommendation. A plausible-looking output is not the same as a defensible one, and HR is the department where the gap between the two costs the most.

The practical takeaway

Delegate the scheduling, drafting, and aggregated pattern-spotting that touches no individual's outcome directly; never delegate a hiring decision, a disciplinary or termination decision, a grievance investigation, or an individual's protected personal data.
