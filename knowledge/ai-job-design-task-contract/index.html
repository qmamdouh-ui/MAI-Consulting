Hiring and job design for an AI-fluent team: what belongs in the job description

A job description silent on AI leaves every hire to decide alone. The Task Contract sorts each role's recurring tasks into own, direct and verify, and gives interviews, appraisals and onboarding a common basis.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Take the last job description your organisation published for a programme officer, a communications lead or a finance assistant. Read it as a stranger would. It probably lists responsibilities written years ago, a set of qualifications, and a closing line about being "comfortable with technology". If a candidate asked what they would be expected to do with AI on a Tuesday afternoon, the document would have no answer.

That gap matters more than it seems. A job description is the earliest and most durable statement an organisation makes about how work is done. It shapes who applies, what is tested at interview, what an appraisal rewards, and what a manager feels entitled to ask for. If it is silent on AI, the organisation has decided by omission, and the decision will be made again, differently, by every individual hire.

The unit of AI value is a task, so the unit of job design has to become the task too.

Why the standard job description fails

Most descriptions list duties as nouns: "manages donor reporting", "coordinates stakeholder communications". Duties like these hide the tasks inside them, and it is the tasks that AI changes. Drafting a first version, summarising a long document, reformatting a table and checking a figure against a source are all different tasks with different risk profiles. A duty that bundles them cannot say which are delegated, which are reviewed, and which must stay entirely human.

The result is a familiar split. Some staff quietly use AI for everything they can. Others avoid it entirely, because nothing tells them it is welcome. Both are rational responses to a document that does not say.

The Task Contract

We suggest replacing the duty list with what we call a Task Contract: a short table attached to every role that sorts its recurring tasks into three columns.

  • Own. Tasks the person performs directly, without AI, because the judgement, relationship or accountability cannot be handed over. Naming these protects the parts of the role that make it distinctive.
  • Direct. Tasks where the person instructs AI and takes responsibility for the result. This is where description, delegation and discernment are exercised, and where the role gains capacity.
  • Verify. Tasks where AI output arrives from others, or from a standing workflow, and the person's job is to check it before it moves on. Review is real work and should be written down as such.

A Task Contract usually runs to half a page. It gives the candidate a truthful picture, gives the manager a basis for appraisal, and gives the organisation a record of how each role has actually changed.

What to test at interview

Once the role is described in tasks, the interview can test the same things. Asking a candidate whether they are "familiar with AI tools" produces confident answers and little information. A better approach uses a short, realistic exercise built from the role's own Direct and Verify columns.

Give the candidate a messy source document and an AI-drafted summary that contains one plausible but wrong statement. Ask them to describe how they would brief the tool for the first version, and then to review the draft aloud. What you learn is whether they can specify a task clearly, and whether they notice what is wrong. Neither depends on knowing a particular product, and both predict how the person will work in the role.

We would avoid two shortcuts. Do not screen for prompt tricks, which change quickly and reveal little. And do not treat a candidate's refusal to use AI as disqualifying by default; ask what they would use instead, and why. Sometimes the answer is a thoughtful description of a risk your own policy has not yet addressed.

A candidate who can say what they would not delegate, and why, tells you more than one who can list every tool they have tried.

Junior and senior roles need different contracts

The same framework produces different results at different levels. For an entry-level role, the Direct column will be long, and the Verify column short. The risk is that the person never learns the underlying craft, which is a question of development rather than hiring, covered in the companion piece on the apprenticeship problem.

For a senior role, the balance reverses. Most of the value lies in Own and Verify: deciding what should be done, and checking that what comes back is sound. An organisation that describes a senior post in terms of drafting output has misunderstood what it is buying.

Changing existing roles without a restructure

Nothing here requires advertising new posts. The same Task Contract can be drafted for existing roles in a single team meeting. Each person lists the ten tasks they repeat most often, and the group sorts them into the three columns together.

Two things tend to follow. People discover that they already Direct AI for several tasks and welcome the chance to say so openly. And managers discover tasks that were assumed to be Own but are really Verify, because the work arrives half-formed from a colleague or a tool. Both discoveries are useful, and neither needs a consultant.

Handle the outcome with care. A Task Contract that is later used to justify cutting posts will be gamed, and the honest information it contains will disappear. Say plainly, before starting, what the exercise is for and what it will not be used for.

Making it stick

Three habits keep the contract alive. Review it at each appraisal, so that it follows the work rather than the other way round. Include one line about AI-related responsibilities in every new job advertisement, however brief. And give whoever owns onboarding a standing item to walk each new hire through their contract in the first week.

None of this is expensive. It is a change of vocabulary, from duties to tasks, applied consistently at the points where the organisation already writes down what it expects.

Questions for your next leadership meeting

  • Could a candidate reading our current job descriptions tell what we expect them to do with AI, and what we expect them not to?
  • Which tasks in our most common roles have quietly moved from Own to Direct, and does anyone with authority know?
  • What would we test at interview if we had to identify how a person specifies and checks work, rather than which tools they know?

The bottom line

Describe every role as tasks you expect people to own, direct or verify, and hire, appraise and develop against that description. An AI-fluent team is not recruited by asking for fluency. It is built by saying, in writing, what the work now is.
