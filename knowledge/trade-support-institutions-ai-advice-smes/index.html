Trade-support institutions advising SMEs on AI: five moves that hold up

Chambers, export councils and trade promotion agencies are asked about AI by small firms. Five moves give the advice a hold: start from a task, agree one data rule, teach one workflow per sector, convene peers and measure.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Trade promotion agencies, chambers of commerce, export councils and business associations sit closer to small and medium-sized enterprises than almost any other institution. When an exporter with fifteen staff wonders whether AI is worth the trouble, this is who they are likely to ask. That places these institutions in an uncomfortable position. They are expected to have an answer, and most were not built to give technology advice.

The temptation is to respond with an event. A seminar on AI for exporters, a list of recommended tools, a webinar with a vendor. These are easy to organise and leave no lasting change in how the businesses work. We think there is a better way to spend the same effort, and it starts with asking what advice is still useful six months after the adviser has left the room.

Advice to a small business is worth what survives once the adviser has stopped talking.

Why SME advice on AI is different

A small firm has no IT department, no policy team and rarely any spare time. The owner is often also the sales lead, the accountant and the person who answers the phone. Advice that assumes a governance committee or a dedicated project lead will simply not be applied. At the same time, small firms handle real customer, supplier and pricing data, and a careless habit costs them more than it costs a large organisation with layers of protection.

Advisers also face a credibility problem. An institution that recommends specific products may be seen as endorsing them, and one that says nothing may be seen as absent. The advice has to be useful without becoming a sales channel.

The Five Holds

We use the idea of a hold, meaning the thing that keeps advice in place after the session ends. Five moves give advice a hold in a small business.

  • Start from a task, not a tool. Ask the owner what took the most time last week: a quotation, a customs document, a supplier email in another language. Advice that begins there connects to something the owner already cares about. Advice that begins with a product name does not.
  • Agree one data rule. Small firms need one sentence, not a policy: what customer, supplier and price information may never be pasted into a public tool. A rule the owner can repeat to a new hire is worth more than a page nobody reads.
  • Teach one workflow per sector. A garment exporter and a food processor do not share a daily routine. Pick one repeatable task in each sector, show it end to end, and let others copy it. Breadth comes from repeating the pattern, not from a longer menu.
  • Build a peer cohort. Small firms trust other small firms. A group of five or six businesses that meet briefly each month to compare what worked will do more than any single training. The institution's role is to convene, not to lecture.
  • Measure and hand back. Ask each firm to note one measure before and after, such as time to prepare a quotation. Collect the results and share them anonymously. This produces the evidence that later advice can rely on.

What the institution should not do

Some habits look helpful and weaken the advice. Recommending a single vendor as the answer creates dependency and reputational risk. Presenting AI as a competitiveness necessity, without evidence for the firm in question, produces anxiety rather than judgement. And promising savings without measurement invites disappointment that lands on the institution.

There is also the matter of honesty. Sometimes the right advice is that a firm does not need AI for a particular problem, and that a better spreadsheet or a clearer process would serve it better. An adviser who can say so is more trusted the next time.

The most useful thing a trade-support body can give a small firm is one habit it will still have in a year.

Capability inside the institution first

Advisers cannot pass on what they do not practise. Before advising members, the institution's own staff need enough fluency to know what good looks like, where the tools fail, and what data must stay out. This does not require a large programme. A short period in which advisers use the tools on their own routine work, and compare notes, is often enough to change the quality of the conversations they have with members.

It also protects the institution. Advice given without that grounding tends to repeat vendor claims, and a member who is later disappointed will remember who said it.

Multilingual and cross-border realities

Members of trade-support institutions often work across languages and borders, and many of their daily tasks involve translation, regulatory paperwork and correspondence with partners abroad. These are natural candidates for AI-assisted workflows, and also natural places for errors that carry commercial or legal consequences. A translated contract clause or a mistaken tariff description is not a cosmetic slip.

Good advice in this setting pairs the workflow with a review step: someone fluent in the target language, or familiar with the regulation, checks the output before it leaves the firm. The institution can support this by keeping a short list of trusted reviewers, which costs little and solves a problem members otherwise carry alone.

A practical starting point

Pick one sector and one task within it. Recruit five member firms. Run three short sessions over three months: the first to agree the task and the data rule, the second to compare what happened, the third to record the before and after measures. Publish the results in a page. Then repeat with a second sector, using what was learned.

This is modest, and it produces three things an event cannot: a working example, a small body of evidence, and a group of firms that talk to each other.

Questions for your next leadership meeting

  • Which of our members' tasks would benefit from advice, and how do we know?
  • Do our own advisers use these tools on their own work, or only recommend them?
  • What would we need to see to say to a member that AI is not the right answer to their problem?

The bottom line

Give small firms one task, one data rule and one peer group, and measure the result; that is advice with a hold. An institution that leaves behind a habit and some evidence has done more than one that leaves behind a slide deck.
