AI-assisted research a programme officer can defend

How a programme officer can use AI to surface sources faster while keeping every claim traceable: quote the passage, open each source, and treat suggested citations as leads until verified.

By M.A.I. Consulting

This series opens a new run on role-specific patterns, starting with one of the highest-stakes uses of AI in this sector: research. A programme officer scanning the literature for a proposal, or pulling precedent for a landscape review, is producing something whose entire value lies in its evidentiary weight. Get the drafting wrong and you redo a paragraph. Get the research wrong and a citation that does not actually say what your document claims it says can surface months later, in a funder's own fact-check, with your name attached to it.

This piece is about the specific workflow habits that keep AI-assisted research defensible, meaning every claim in it can be traced back to a source someone can actually open and check.

Why research is a different case of "check the source"

The last piece in this series named checking the source as one of the four essential checks, and research-assist is that check pushed to its limit. Most delegated tasks fail quietly into mediocre output. Research fails specifically into confident, plausible-sounding claims attributed to sources that were summarised loosely, misremembered, or in the worst case never actually existed. A drafting error costs a rewrite. A fabricated citation, once discovered, costs the credibility of the entire document it sits in.

What "defensible" actually requires

Defensible does not mean the AI's summary sounded reasonable. It means a specific person, opening the specific source, can find the specific claim in it. That standard is higher than most people apply to their own reading notes, and it is the standard research has to meet the moment it appears under someone's name in a document going to a funder or a board.

Workflow patterns that keep research traceable

  • Ask the model to quote the passage, not just cite the source. A quoted sentence can be searched for and checked against the original text in seconds; a bare citation with no quoted text gives you nothing to verify against.
  • Verify every citation before it leaves your hands, not after. This is the "check the source" habit from the last piece applied literally: open the source, confirm the quote exists and means what the draft claims, before the citation goes anywhere near a document with your name on it.
  • Treat AI-suggested sources as leads, not evidence, until opened. A suggested reference is a place to look, not a fact you can already cite; the distinction matters most under deadline pressure, which is exactly when it gets forgotten.
  • Keep your own working notes on what was AI-suggested and what you verified yourself. This trail can stay private and informal, but it is what lets you reconstruct your own process if a citation is challenged later, rather than having to take it on faith that you checked something months ago.

A citation you have not opened yourself is a lead, not evidence.

The trade-off in verifying every citation

Opening and checking every AI-suggested source takes real time, often as long as finding the source yourself would have taken, which can make the research-assist step feel like it saved nothing. The honest answer is that the saving is real but sits elsewhere: the AI is genuinely faster at surfacing candidate sources and summarising what to look for, even though the checking time afterwards is not optional and does not shrink just because the first pass was quick.

The practical takeaway

Use AI to surface candidate sources and draft summaries quickly, but treat every suggested citation as an unverified lead until you have personally opened the source and confirmed the quoted claim is actually there; nothing shortcuts that step for research that will carry your name.
