AI in crisis and humanitarian contexts: fast deployment with high-stakes data

In an emergency there is no time to decide how to use AI. Sort tasks in advance by speed and stakes, classify crisis data before it arrives, and name who answers AI questions within the hour.

By M.A.I. Consulting

Imagine a field office in the first week of a sudden emergency. It is an illustrative scene, not a real one. Registration lists are growing by the hour, partner messages arrive in three languages, and a coordinator has a report due to headquarters by morning. Someone suggests using an AI tool to summarise the incoming messages and draft the situation update. It would save hours. It would also mean pasting names, locations and health information into a system nobody has assessed.

There is no time to convene a committee. There is also no undo button for data about people who are displaced, injured or at risk of reprisal. This is the tension that crisis contexts bring to AI adoption: the pressure to move fast is at its highest exactly when the cost of a data mistake is at its highest.

In a crisis you do not have time to decide how to use AI, so the decisions have to be made before the crisis begins.

Why calm-time rules fail under pressure

Most organisations write AI policies for ordinary conditions: a stable office, time to consult, a compliance officer within reach. These rules are sensible and they collapse in an emergency, because they assume a decision process the crisis does not allow. Staff then face a choice between waiting for permission, which may cost lives or time, and improvising, which may expose vulnerable people.

Improvisation is the more likely outcome. A person under pressure will use the tool that is nearest and works. If nothing has been prepared, the choice is made by convenience, not by judgement.

The Speed-Stakes Grid

We suggest organisations that work in or near crises sort their tasks in advance on two questions: how quickly does this need to be done, and how much harm could follow if the data or the output is wrong or exposed? Placing tasks on the resulting grid gives four zones with different standing instructions.

  • Fast and low stakes. Tasks such as drafting a meeting agenda, formatting a template or translating a public notice. Pre-approve these. Staff should not need to ask.
  • Fast and high stakes. Tasks such as summarising messages from affected people or triaging reports. Allow only with pre-approved tools, minimum data, and a named person who reviews the output before it goes anywhere. This is the zone where preparation matters most.
  • Slow and low stakes. Tasks such as background research or lessons-learned drafts. Normal review applies, and there is time to check.
  • Slow and high stakes. Tasks such as case decisions or protection referrals. Keep these with people. If AI assists at all, it does so under the full ordinary process, because there is time to use it.

The value of the grid is not the precision of the sorting. It is that the conversation about which zone a task belongs to happens in calm, not in the first week of an emergency.

Data is the deciding factor

In humanitarian settings, the data matter more than the tool. The OCHA Data Responsibility Guidelines, revised in January 2025, describe data responsibility as the safe, ethical and effective management of personal and non-personal data for operational response, and the revision addresses emerging technologies including artificial intelligence. That framing is useful: it places the question where it belongs, on what is done with the data, not on which product is fashionable.

For most organisations the practical translation is a short classification prepared in advance. Which categories of information may never enter an external AI tool, such as identifiable data on affected people? Which may enter with minimisation, such as removing names and locations before a summary? Which are freely usable, such as public reports? Staff in a crisis should be able to look this up in seconds.

A crisis is the worst moment to discover what your data classification would have said.

Preparing before it happens

Preparation is inexpensive compared with the alternative, and it comes down to a few items. A one-page data classification for crisis use, written in plain language. A short list of pre-assessed tools with the tasks each may be used for. A named person, with a deputy, who can answer a question about AI use within the hour. And a simple log, kept during the crisis, of what was used for what, so that the response can later be reviewed and improved.

It also helps to rehearse. A short scenario exercise, perhaps once a year, in which a team decides which zone of the grid a handful of realistic tasks fall into, surfaces disagreements while they are cheap to resolve.

Partners, contractors and local staff

Crises involve many actors: local partners, contractors, volunteers, surge staff who arrive knowing little about the organisation's rules. The standing instructions need to reach them. That means a briefing that fits into the first day, and tool arrangements that do not depend on each person's private account. Where local partners have their own practices, the aim is to agree what is acceptable for shared data, not to impose a rule that cannot be followed.

Language deserves attention as well. Messages from affected communities may be in languages where AI tools perform unevenly, and a confident but wrong summary can misdirect a response. Review by someone who knows the language and the context is a control.

What to record for afterwards

Organisations are rightly asked, after a response, how decisions were made. A light record of AI use during the crisis, kept as it happens, answers a question that will otherwise be answered from memory. It should note the task, the tool, the data category, and who reviewed the output. It need not be elaborate. It needs to exist.

Questions for your next leadership meeting

  • If an emergency began tomorrow, which AI-related decisions would staff make on their own, and by what rule?
  • Do we have a crisis data classification that a new arrival could apply on day one?
  • Who can answer an AI-use question within an hour, and does everyone know who that is?

The bottom line

Sort tasks by speed and stakes now, classify your data in advance, and name who decides, so that speed in a crisis never means improvising with other people's lives. Preparation is the only form of speed that does not borrow against the safety of the people you serve.
