What AI fluency actually means: the 4Ds after a hundred days of practice

AI fluency is not tool knowledge, speed or confidence. It is one habit, ask for something specific and check it against what you know, and the 4Ds show where that habit takes real work.

By Mamdouh Alqudsi

"AI fluency" is used to describe everything from prompt-writing tricks to full organisational transformation, so it is hard to know what you are meant to be building in yourself or your team. After several months of writing about AI in nonprofit and trade-support work, the honest answer is much smaller than the phrase suggests, and more useful for being small.

What fluency is not

  • It is not tool knowledge. Knowing every feature of every AI product does not make you fluent, any more than knowing every keyboard shortcut in a word processor makes you a good writer.
  • It is not speed. Producing AI-assisted output quickly is a different skill from producing output that is correct, and the gap only shows after something has gone out.
  • It is not confidence. Sounding sure about what a model can do is often inversely related to how carefully someone has checked its output.

Fluency in anything is the ability to use it correctly without thinking hard about the mechanics. With AI, the mechanics that matter are judgement, not syntax.

What it is: one habit

Fluency is a small, repeatable habit: know what the task needs, ask for exactly that, and check the result against something real before it goes anywhere. Asked which single piece of advice mattered most across dozens of situations (donor updates, grant reports, translation, training programmes, starter kits), the answer was not a tactic. It was this habit, sitting underneath almost every case.

It lasts because:

  • It works with any tool, this year's or next year's, which is why it outlasts advice tied to a particular product.
  • It separates a wasted subscription from a real habit. In the cases behind this guide, organisations that got value asked for something specific and checked the result; stalled rollouts skipped one of the two steps.
  • It can be taught in five minutes, unlike a starter kit or a training programme.

Someone with only this habit can get value on day one. They ask the tool to draft a specific email instead of "help with communications", and read what comes back sceptically before sending it. That avoids the two failures that recur most: vague requests that produce vague, unused output, and confident wrong answers that go out unchecked.

The 4Ds, reread after practice

The 4Ds are Anthropic's AI Fluency Framework: Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence. On first reading they look like a tidy list. After a hundred-plus days and dozens of real tasks, the four words stay the same, but the weight each one carries changes.

  • Delegation turned out to be the easy one. Deciding what to hand off got faster within weeks, because a short list of recurring tasks made it obvious which belonged to a tool and which did not.
  • Description never stopped improving. Every stalled task traced back to this D more than any other. Being specific enough, every time, stayed the hardest habit to keep.
  • Discernment needs more than one sentence. "Read output like an editor" is easy advice and a hard habit to hold when the output sounds confident and the task is boring. The gap between advice and practice only shows with repetition.
  • Diligence is the quiet one. The first three appear the moment a task starts: what to hand off, how to ask, what to check. Diligence appears later, when something goes out under your name and a question comes back about where it came from, whether from a donor, a colleague or a supervisor. It gets the least attention early and turns out to carry the most consequence.

Specific description and honest discernment are the two halves of the one habit above. Delegation decides where to use it; Diligence is owning the result afterwards.

What to do next

  • Write down, in one sentence, the specific task you will hand to an AI tool this week and what a correct result looks like.
  • Before you send anything the tool produced, check it against something you already know to be true.
  • Pick the D you find hardest (for most people, Description or Discernment) and practise it deliberately on one recurring task for a month.
  • For anything that goes out under your name, decide now how you would answer "where did this come from?"
  • Continue with the guides on prompting in short, checked steps and on calibrating trust in AI output.
