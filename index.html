Who this is for

Mission-driven and international organisations of 20 to 500 staff: NGOs, foundations, UN agencies and public institutions with no in-house AI function and with beneficiary, donor or personal data in their daily work. We talk first to the executive director or the data protection officer.

Who it is not for

Under 20 staff, a free course will serve you better and we will say so. Private-sector work is taken on referral only. We build no AI systems. Foundations funding a portfolio of grantees have their own page.

The problem, with the evidence

Your programme, fundraising and operations staff are already drafting donor reports with consumer AI tools this week, some of them with beneficiary data open in the next tab.

There are no rules that permit it, no training built for their actual role, and nothing to send when a funder asks. The three cards below give the evidence and what it costs.

It happens every week

Every proposal, every donor report, every board paper. 76% of UK charities now use AI tools, up from 61% a year earlier. 92% of US nonprofits report some use, and 81% of that use is ad hoc and undocumented.

Charity Digital Skills Report 2025 (672 charities); Nonprofit AI Adoption Report 2026 (346 nonprofits).

What it costs

Only 7% of nonprofits report major impact from AI. 88% of professionals have put work information into public chatbots, a third of it customer or beneficiary data. When a funder's due-diligence pack asks about AI governance, an organisation with no policy has nothing to send.

Shadow-AI surveys 2026; CEP AI With Purpose, 2025.

How organisations cope today

They tolerate it. Or they buy a catalogue licence, after which over 90% of staff still feel unprepared. Or IT circulates a “don't” memo and use goes underground. 47% still have no AI governance policy. Among those without one, the barriers named are training (48%) and not knowing where to start (44%).

The current way of coping is the real competitor. It has a row in the comparison table.

The method behind every service

Four words hold the five services together.

Delegation, Description, Discernment and Diligence: the AI Fluency framework published by Anthropic with Rick Dakan and Joseph Feller. The framework is public, so you can check our method against it.

Delegation

What to hand to AI, what to keep human, and who decides.

Description

How to ask, with your organisation's context already in the request.

Discernment

How to check an output before anyone relies on it.

Diligence

What to log, what to disclose, and who owns the result.

They are the four axes of your assessment score, the four sections of every team's cheatsheet, the four clause groups of your policy and the four modules of every session. The diagnosis, the training and the rules use one vocabulary, so a gap found in the assessment maps directly onto a clause and a lab.
